# San Carlo alle Quattro Fontane: a bounded object reading

**Object:** Church within a Trinitarian convent complex, Via del Quirinale 23,
Rome, Italy. **Architect:** Francesco Borromini; the facade was completed after
his death by his nephew Bernardo Castelli. **Context:** Roman Baroque.
The church operator verifies the commission and phased authorship, rather than
a single undifferentiated completion date.
[Operator chronology](https://sancarlino.org/historia-it/cronologia/),
[facade account](https://sancarlino.org/espacios/la-facciata/).

## Identity and historical limits

The operator's chronology gives the commission in 1634, convent/cloister work
from 1635, church construction from 1638 and completion of the complex in 1644.
Its separate church account summarizes construction as 1634–1641; Rome's tourism
authority summarizes an initial 1638–1642 period. These summaries have different
boundaries and do not establish one exact completion date for every part.
[Chronology](https://sancarlino.org/historia-it/cronologia/),
[church account](https://sancarlino.org/espacios/la-chiesa/),
[Rome tourism authority](https://www.turismoroma.it/en/places/church-san-carlo-alle-quattro-fontane).

The lower facade tier was erected by Francesco before his death in 1667;
Bernardo completed the upper tier. The operator dates individual sculptures
to 1675 and 1682, while its chronology summarizes facade/bell-tower decoration
under 1680. Consequently, neither “built in 1641” nor “entirely completed by
Francesco in 1667” describes the present ensemble adequately. The later floor
finish and photographed fittings also should not be assumed original merely
because they appear in a present interior photograph.
[Facade](https://sancarlino.org/espacios/la-facciata/),
[chronology](https://sancarlino.org/historia-it/cronologia/).

This history matters architecturally: the street elevation and the interior
cannot be treated as one simultaneous operation, and a visible stylistic family
does not imply identical authorship or construction phase.

## What the inspected material supports

**Plan observation — book evidence.** In P15, file page 233 / printed page 218,
the church is one part of an irregularly bounded complex. Its elongated interior
has alternating perimeter recesses and projections. A cloister lies beside it,
with connecting rooms and a garden beyond; the church is neither the full plot
nor a free-standing ellipse. The plan has no usable dimensional scale in this
reading. It supports relative arrangement, not measured room capacities or a
reconstruction of the geometry. Source identity and pagination are recorded
[below](#evidence-and-limits).

**Facade observation — operator photograph.** Two stacked tiers, continuous
projecting horizontal bands and repeated columns organize a shallow street
front. Side fields retreat while the entrance field advances; the upper central
opening and large oval frame complicate a simple repetition of the lower tier.
The photo is near-frontal and cannot establish the whole approach or hidden
wall construction.
[Inspected facade still](https://www.sancarlino.es/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/Facciata.jpg),
linked from the [operator's account](https://sancarlino.org/espacios/la-facciata/).

**Interior observation — operator photograph.** The altar view shows a light,
largely continuous wall surface, a strong cornice curving around the room,
columns framing openings, and a layered arch/pendentive transition above.
The image crops the upper dome and does not show the whole occupied floor.
It verifies visible continuity and articulation, not a measured daylight effect.
[Inspected altar/intermediate-zone still](https://sancarlino.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/ALTAR-SAN-CARLINO-DESPUES-DE-LA-REFORMA.jpg).

**Source description, not independently reconstructed geometry.** The operator
describes an elongated Greek-cross starting scheme reshaped by concave and
convex curves, with a separate transition to an elliptical dome. It also
identifies brick construction with a plastered finish. Rome's account describes
the continuous entablature and diminishing dome coffers. “Oval” is therefore a
useful description of the dominant interior field, but is insufficient for the
wall perimeter, dome support zone or complete complex. No exact circle centers,
triangular construction, ellipse equation or governing proportional ratio was
verified here.
[Church](https://sancarlino.org/espacios/la-chiesa/),
[Rome account](https://www.turismoroma.it/en/places/church-san-carlo-alle-quattro-fontane).

## Interpretation and competing explanations

The following are original architectural interpretations. They remain hypotheses
about how the inspected relationships work, rather than claims about documented
design intent or every visitor's experience.

| Reading | Causal interpretation | Alternative and discriminating check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Complex versus central room | Distinguishing the concentrated public interior from adjacent smaller spaces allows different spatial characters within a restricted enclosure. The plan supports this relationship more securely than the familiar facade image. | A regular rectangular hall could preserve more furnishing flexibility. Compare actual occupied clearances and the rooms left beside the hall; curvature alone does not prove efficient use of a small site. |
| Perimeter and spatial continuity | A cornice can connect varied local recesses into one perceived boundary. Columns and openings provide intermediate rhythm without requiring all wall segments to be equal. | Separate rooms may be necessary when activities conflict. Check whether visual continuity is desirable before reproducing it; a continuous enclosure is no substitute for separation. |
| Upward sequence | A differentiated lower order, intermediate transition and upper enclosure can produce hierarchy in section while the plan stays compact. | A single high room may offer enough hierarchy with fewer interfaces. Check the section from the entrance and principal occupied positions; do not infer height ratios from a perspective photograph. |
| Facade depth and entrance | Actual concavity and projection can make the threshold legible through shadow and oblique viewing, while a repeated order connects it to the side fields. | A deep entry recess in a flatter front may perform the same orientation task. Trace the route along the real street and test the door's visibility; this photograph alone cannot settle that comparison. |
| Material expression | The distinction between a construction substrate and a continuous modeled finish allows spatial articulation to be read across individual units. | Exposed unit masonry or a framed lining would establish different joints and repair logic. Identify the actual support and finish interfaces before claiming one method can reproduce the result. |

The adjacent cloister in the book plan is a useful counterexample **within the
same complex**: a different repeated enclosure can coexist with the church's
concentrated interior. Borrowing one perimeter for every room would erase the
distinction that makes the larger composition intelligible. Likewise, the
transverse oval described at Bernini's Sant'Andrea al Quirinale shows why an
oval does not itself determine the arrival direction.
[Sant'Andrea, Rome tourism authority](https://turismoroma.it/en/places/church-santandrea-al-quirinale).

## A small changed brief

**Hypothetical commission:** Convert a modest urban shell into a neighborhood
meeting place with one shared event room, two bookable quiet rooms, storage
and an inclusive entrance. The shell is assumed rectangular, with a narrow
street frontage and a rear opening. Its dimensions, jurisdiction and existing
construction are not established. This is an original decision example, not
a layout fitted to a surveyed property.

**Proposal A — concentrated shared destination:** Put a sheltered threshold and
small arrival space before a regular event room. Use one continuous upper datum
to connect its doors and a shallow recess for waiting; place quiet rooms beside
a separate access route so their users need not cross an event. Reserve the
rear opening for a useful occupied relationship rather than automatically
placing an altar-like focal object there. A modest change of ceiling height at
the shared room may express arrival if the shell and environmental strategy
allow it. No oval or source dimension is transferred.

**Proposal B — equal rooms on a short route:** Keep the event room and quiet
rooms independently legible off one plain entrance route, using repeated door
and ceiling relationships. This reduces the special destination effect and may
be preferable when independent bookings matter more than a communal ceremony.

| Changed condition | Provisional choice and its test |
| --- | --- |
| Meetings and an event occur together | Compare arrivals, queues, sound boundaries and storage delivery. Reject A if its arrival space becomes an unavoidable event spill area for quiet-room users. |
| A second entrance becomes available | Compare a more direct route to quiet rooms with the original concentrated arrival. Check control and staffing; a unified visual front need not force one operational entrance. |
| Cold, wet exposure replaces the Roman setting | Keep the primary enclosure simple and test a sheltered, weather-controlled threshold. Place difficult relief indoors if external cleaning and water shedding remain unresolved. This is an adaptation proposal, not a climatic performance finding. |
| A larger room or lower budget is required | Compare a regular enclosure with one local crafted threshold against continuous curved walls. Check usable area, ceiling/service reservations and component/finish quantities before selecting; no cost or span performance is assumed. |

The transferable mechanism is **a legible common destination held together by
section and edge rhythm, with adjacent spaces allowed to differ**. It fails when
ceremonial emphasis consumes the accessible route, when the dominant room
prevents independent use, or when the section cannot accommodate necessary
construction and servicing. The simpler alternative remains a valid result.
