## Identity and evidence

**Object:** Pantheon, Piazza della Rotonda, Rome, Italy; ancient Roman monumental
architecture within the classical family, subsequently used as a Christian
church. Rome's municipal [monument account](../../../../sources/precedents.md#w-045)
dates Hadrian's reconstruction to **118–125 CE**, distinguishes it from
Agrippa's earlier commission and records conversion in **609**. These dates
describe the selected public account, not a resolved archaeological chronology
for every component. **Architect: not securely established here.** The inspected
municipal account identifies patrons and a dedication; it does not establish a
named designer. Do not promote Agrippa, Hadrian or Apollodorus to a verified
architect on that basis.

**Actually inspected:** the municipal page's historical and architectural
paragraphs and [close exterior photograph](../../../../sources/precedents.md#w-046)
of the portico; library P15 file **108 / printed 93**, cutaway; **211 / 196**,
plan/section; **305 / 290**, regulating-line plan. The printed numbers were
visually checked. These book views establish a represented arrangement, not a
measured survey or proof that the visible interior belongs to one ancient
phase. No on-site inspection, original survey, current full interior photograph
or material investigation was performed. The portico photograph cannot prove
the rotunda's unseen plan.

## Arrangement observed in the inspected representations

The P15 plan places a columned rectangular front before a circular room, joined
through a narrower transition. Recesses articulate a thick perimeter while
the central floor remains a continuous field. Its section distinguishes the
lower entrance volume from the domed room and shows an opening at the crown.
The cutaway makes the coffer rhythm, perimeter articulation and separation
between interior surface and outer mass legible. The regulating-line drawing
adds an analytical geometric overlay; that overlay is not evidence of an
ancient setting-out procedure.

In the municipal photograph, the repeated columns sit forward of a deeply
shadowed threshold; capitals, horizontal bands and the pediment establish
different scales of articulation. This view supports discussing depth and
cadence, while perspective prevents reliable spacing or height measurement.
The municipal text describes a concrete dome and thick supporting masonry;
neither the photograph nor the book cutaway verifies present structural behavior.

## Architectural interpretation

The building can be read as two orders of attention. The front gathers a
directed approach; the circular room redirects attention inward and upward.
The transition matters as much as the dome: entering through a restricted
threshold makes the shared volume perceptibly different. Around that volume,
repeated recesses and vertical elements subdivide a large boundary without
necessarily dividing the main floor. This is an interpretation of the inspected
arrangement, not a recovered account of ancient ceremonies.

The useful proportional question is how entrance height, room breadth and
overhead extent establish hierarchy. It is not whether an unrelated project
can reproduce an ideal sphere. Likewise, the contrast between a highly
articulated public front and the enclosing mass suggests that different parts
can carry different representational work. A modern finish that suggests
heavy masonry would still need its own support, joints and weathering logic.

## Original transfer, failed version and correction

**Hypothetical inputs:** a small neighborhood assembly building, one street
entrance, a wet winter climate, a main hall, two independently booked rooms and
a restricted roof budget. These are new project assumptions, not facts about
the Pantheon.

Version A takes the center literally: a round hall receives all routes from a
monumental porch. It makes the gathering room easy to find but requires people
to cross it to reach the booked rooms. Closing the hall breaks those journeys;
the curved edge also complicates storage and the chosen furniture layout.
The precedent's apparent central clarity has therefore produced a program
failure.

Version B retains a sheltered threshold, a perceptible transition and one
dominant shared room, but uses a rectangular hall with a distribution edge
beside it. The two rooms open from that edge. A higher central ceiling and
selective roof light can provide hierarchy without a concrete dome or an open
oculus. Compare this with a simpler side-lit hall: roof light is justified only
if its useful light, weathering, heat-loss and maintenance consequences support
the project. No environmental result is inferred from the historic example.

**Provisional decision:** choose B if independent bookings remain a fixed
requirement. Check an occupied event plus a late arrival, a closed hall,
accessible arrival in rain, furniture removal, acoustic separation and the
roof/envelope section. Retain the threshold-to-room relationship only if those
states work. At a tighter budget, spend on a durable sheltered doorway and
coherent opening rhythm; simplify ceiling articulation. At a larger scale,
test several controlled room groups rather than expanding one compulsory hub.

## Corrections and reuse limits

P15 captions the Pantheon **120–124** and associates the portico with an earlier
temple. This study uses its visible geometry for composition and the municipal
chronology above for the stated historical basis; it does not adopt the caption
as component-level archaeological evidence. The front inscription also cannot
resolve authorship. A dated patron, a familiar name and a geometric diagram are
three different kinds of evidence.

The transfer is independently authored. No plan, dimensions, image or book text
is reproduced. Source access does not establish redistribution permission.
