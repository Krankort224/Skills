# Mass, void and massing operations

Start from the brief, [actual demand](../../program/demand.md) and accepted decisions. Choose and compare architectural consequences rather than inferring a style or regulation from a name; select [requirements](../../requirements.md) where applicability matters.

## How should mass and void organize the site and rooms?

**Decision:** Distribute occupied parts, outdoor voids and building edges to
support the chosen parti and site relationships. Study these in plan and, where
needed, conceptual section or elevation; this is architectural composition,
not a building model, structural design or verified environmental analysis.

**Inputs:** Site reading, program areas and adjacencies, plausible heights or
levels where known, verified site constraints and the parti alternatives.

1. Start from the major program group and add other parts in their required
   relationships; alternatively start from a provisional available site area
   and remove space for entry, outdoor use or a valued view. The latter is not a
   legally buildable envelope unless all relevant constraints have been verified.
2. Examine the resulting solids and voids together. Does the outdoor space
   have a purpose, access, defined edges and a relationship to its users, or is
   it left over? Does splitting the footprint create useful connections or an
   exposed, difficult-to-maintain gap?
3. Compare one compact arrangement with a more articulated one at the same
   program assumptions. A compact form may reduce exposed perimeter and simplify
   routes but produce deeper rooms; separated wings may offer more external edges
   and identity but lengthen travel and increase exposed junctions. Neither
   implies a predicted energy or daylight result.
4. At the public edge, compare aligning with a neighboring datum, stepping back,
   or creating a gap. At a slope, compare adapting the footprint to contours
   with consolidating levels; mark access and earthwork implications for later
   verification. For views, locate observer and target in plan and section,
   then compare retaining a gap, framing a view or accepting an obstruction.
5. Test the chosen footprint with representative rooms, circulation, openings,
   support and service zones. Note awkward corners, deep interiors, long routes,
   complicated roof or drainage junctions, and interfaces requiring specialist
   review. Return to the parti if these changes undermine its purpose.

### What the massing operations change

**Add volumes** when distinct activities need different heights, orientations or
operating identities. Begin with a critical room or group, add its neighbors and
make their links explicit. The joints can become entries, small courts or service
strips; narrow leftovers without access are failure conditions. Too many separate
roofs and links can weaken the overall order and consume area.

**Carve a volume** when an outer edge, overall compactness or retained fabric
should remain continuous. Subtract a court, entry recess, light slot or terrace
with an identified use. Refit the program after each subtraction: removed area
does not disappear from the brief. A continuous outer shell can conceal awkward
interior depths, so test room bands on both sides of each void.

**Step, shift or taper** when different levels should address different edges,
when a lower datum should relate to neighbors, or when upper mass must withdraw
from a view. These moves change floor area, support alignment, circulation and
roof junctions together. A setback useful in elevation may leave an inaccessible
roof strip; a shifted plate may require an unresolved transfer. Prefer a simpler
alignment when the offset has no spatial benefit.

**Split a mass** when a passage, view or useful outdoor room is more valuable
than continuity. Locate the observer or route and check the gap in section as
well as plan. A slit between tall walls can read as a void in a diagram while
being poor usable ground. Separate parts also need complete arrival, support
and servicing; a glazed link is not area-free.

Compare the *distribution* of mass, not only its total: one tall edge can define
a court and another low edge can open it to sky and neighbors. A high hall beside
lower rooms can make the shared destination legible; equal-height fragments may
make that hierarchy disappear. No universal solid/void percentage establishes
civic character or comfort. Define whether a ratio concerns a site footprint,
section, floor area or facade before using it.

**Worked comparison:** On a corner site, a single compact block can address
both streets with one entrance at the corner, while an L-shaped arrangement can
form a garden behind it. The L gives the garden more enclosure but introduces an
inside corner and may make the second street edge less active. Select only after
checking room access, garden use and the actual site constraints.

For daylight, solar exposure, wind, acoustic and envelope questions, formulate
the architectural alternatives and use [performance and coordination](../../performance/index.md).
No geometry alone proves comfort, code compliance or system capacity.
