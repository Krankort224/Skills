# Occupied bays and facade order

Start from the brief, [actual demand](../../program/demand.md) and accepted decisions. Choose and compare architectural consequences rather than inferring a style or regulation from a name; select [requirements](../../requirements.md) where applicability matters.

## Derive an elevation from occupied bays

A bay is a repeated interval in the building's order; it may be structural, a
room group or a facade subdivision. Keep these grids distinct until coordinated.
Start with a representative room and its wall: where do furniture, occupied
positions, views, doors and partitions leave usable opening locations? Add the
floor/roof edge and plausible support zones. Repeat that relationship along the
frontage, then identify deliberate exceptions for entry, common room, end and
corner. Elevation and plan are developed together rather than wrapping an
unrelated pattern around the plan.

| Opening order | Spatial and expressive consequences | Conflict to resolve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Punched openings in a solid field | Individual apertures make room/bay increments and wall depth legible; piers, heads and sills can establish a common order. Useful for cellular rooms or a desired sense of enclosure. | Centering every opening on a room can produce erratic exterior spacing; forcing exterior regularity can place windows across partitions. Group rooms or allow a reasoned exception. Deep reveals alter view and potential light and need their own enclosure detail. |
| Grouped openings within a larger bay | Several small rooms or one shared room sit within a common surround or recess. Useful for relating fine room increments to a larger urban frontage. | The surround must allow actual room divisions and support. A visually continuous opening may conceal opaque spandrels or private rooms; state those differences rather than depict uninterrupted transparency. |
| Horizontal band | Connect openings or glazing along a floor; emphasizes continuity and can allow several internal room arrangements. | Partitions still need viable junctions; solid panels may be needed at support, storage or privacy zones. Long bands crossing an entry or corner can erase hierarchy. Coordinate the true clear opening and opaque zones in each bay. |
| Expressed frame with infill | Columns, beams or a secondary facade frame order glazed and opaque fields; primary and secondary scales can remain legible. | A visible facade frame is not necessarily the load-bearing frame. Distinguish actual support from applied expression and check alignment, depth and maintenance at their interface. |
| Screen or layered edge | A permeable outer layer, loggia or deep reveal mediates street, privacy and an inner opening; its depth can form an inhabited threshold. | A screen can obstruct view, cleaning, doors or rescue/access provisions; a shallow decorative layer may promise inhabitable depth it does not provide. Show the usable zone and independent inner enclosure. |

Choose repetition when room needs repeat, alternation when two complementary
uses repeat, and progression where an actual transition in activity, exposure
or approach justifies gradual change. Irregularity can record different rooms;
it still needs an intelligible order. On an asymmetrical elevation, balance a
large void or tall volume with the length, depth or density of another part;
equal visual weight does not require mirrored functions.

Check the corner as a meeting of two inhabited edges. Wrapping one rhythm may
squeeze the last room or leave a very narrow end panel. Options include a solid
corner pier, an opening that belongs to one face, a recessed joint, or a special
corner room expressed on both faces. Choose for actual outlook, privacy and
support. Similarly decide how the repeated order ends at ground, parapet, roof
and existing building; do not hide cumulative dimensional mismatch in a sliver.

For cladding expression, distinguish a **joint** from an **opening** and a
**facade panel** from a **room bay**. A quiet elevation can use few repeated panel
families; special panels can concentrate at entries and corners. Similar-looking
panels may have different edge lengths, diagonals, handedness, grain or coating
orientation and therefore may not be interchangeable. In a manual study compare
regular flat units, faceted segments or curved units, counting special conditions
and extra joints as well as surface area. Flat facets change the silhouette and
reflections; smaller units follow a curve more closely but multiply edges and
fixings. A triangular panel is planar, but triangulation increases joint length
and node variety and does not establish the assembly's feasibility. Do not set
fabrication tolerances, stock sizes or cost savings without supplier evidence.

These are compositional and rationalization principles; no panel model,
algorithm, fabrication schedule or engineering design is implied. Coordinate
movement, fixing, water and layer continuity through the enclosure reference.
