# Site and context

Use this knowledge to generate a position, edge and arrival strategy from the
brief. A site boundary is not a verified buildable envelope. Keep observed
conditions, project preferences and statutory restrictions distinct; select
applicable sources through [requirements](../../requirements.md). Missing evidence
allows provisional alternatives, not invented site facts. Methods are adapted
locally; provenance is in [sources](../../../sources/index.md).

## Read the site as relationships

An edge has thickness and uses on both sides. Record where people approach,
pause, enter, look out, deliver, maintain and withdraw, including changes by time
of day. A busy street may supply visibility but also disturbance; a quiet edge
may offer privacy but poor supervision. A neighboring blank wall may tolerate
service uses while its ownership, access and future development remain unknown.
Locate valued views by observer, target and intervening section: an opening that
captures a private view may obstruct a public one. Community preference and
legal protection require their own evidence.

Read urban grain at several scales: street and block continuity, plot widths,
building gaps, heights and roof/cornice lines, entrance spacing and ground-floor
depth. Continue a consequential relationship rather than copy a neighbor's
ornament. A large program can keep one internal room while dividing its frontage
into smaller bays; it need not become several buildings. Conversely, cosmetic
vertical strips cannot make an uninterrupted service wall an active frontage.

## Translate climate and parcel inputs into site moves

Select the actual location, use and work before transferring an exposed porch,
court or roof from a reference. General relationships above remain useful across
jurisdictions; a locality's climate inputs and a parcel's legal envelope require
their own evidence. A hypothetical input is a test condition, not an observed
site fact. For confirmed Russia use the bounded
[climate/site/assembly selection](../../normative/ru/applicability.md#select-climate-site-and-assembly-inputs);
retain other jurisdictions under [requirements](../../requirements.md).

| Input to establish | Architectural consequence to test |
| --- | --- |
| Location-specific seasonal exposure, sun direction/times, wind/rain exposure and periods of intended outdoor use | Compare an enclosed waiting place with an open shaded threshold; locate outdoor activity where the relevant exposure can be tested. A city name or a national climate label cannot choose either. |
| Surveyed levels, water routes, ground and groundwater evidence | Keep an entrance and its waiting area out of a proposed discharge/overflow path; compare one public level with stepped parts using the whole ground section. Do not infer foundation or drainage feasibility from slope alone. |
| Indoor use, humidity, opening hours and heating/cooling mode | Distinguish a continuously conditioned reading room from an occasionally heated pavilion or moisture-producing use. A shared garden does not make their enclosure or service requirements identical. |
| Maintenance method, storage and seasonal operating decisions | Reserve access to roof outlets, shade controls, facade cleaning and equipment replacement; show where cleared material can be held if clearing is part of the assumed operation. Do not put temporary storage in the inclusive route. |
| Actual parcel, permitted use, intervention and protection regimes | Limit the study footprint only with parcel-relevant evidence; record which boundary or parameter changes an option. A city-wide document title is not a confirmed setback, height or heritage restriction. |

For a Russian parcel, match the cadastral boundary to the adopted PZZ text and
zoning sheet, relevant GPZU information and any intersecting zone-establishing
acts through the [local source route](../../normative/ru/territory-and-special-conditions.md#local-discovery-routes).
Identify the act, applicable parcel fragment and date behind a drawn constraint;
compare heritage-object/protection boundaries with the actual works, rather than
assuming that every old building or every parcel in a city has the same regime.
When no parcel is supplied, retain alternative footprints as hypothetical and
leave these selections unresolved. Climate evidence for a city supplies no
parcel rights or protection-zone clearance.

Trace one ordinary arrival and one maintenance operation through each option in
the intended operating season. If weather protection adds a lobby, recount its
area and show door/queue positions; if shading adds an external screen, reserve
its support and cleaning reach. If ground evidence puts the preferred terrace
in a water route, move it or revise the levels/discharge concept before keeping
its claimed use. Carry these physical changes into the room program, facade and
section, rather than merely adding an exposure note to an unchanged plan. The
[two-location study](../../../assets/studies/reading-pavilions.md#two-russian-reading-pavilions-climate-and-use-change-the-edge)
shows this translation with explicit limits.

## Choose how the building meets its edges

| Strategy | How it works; conditions that favor it | Conflicts and failure modes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Continue the street wall | Place occupied frontage on the prevailing edge; entrances and outward-facing uses make a continuous public room with the street. Useful where an established street line and pedestrian destinations matter. | A continuous footprint may deepen rooms or block a through-route. Transparent glass without an occupied use, door or appropriate privacy is weak activation. Reconcile frontage with support and servicing behind it. |
| Set back to make a forecourt | Exchange frontage for a legible waiting/gathering space between street and entrance. Give it a destination, occupied edge and a clear public boundary. Useful for queued arrival or a shared civic entrance. | A setback alone produces leftover paving. Parking, delivery movements or an unmarked access boundary can occupy the intended gathering space. Test the forecourt after doors, furniture and actual arrival flows are placed. |
| Turn an L around an outdoor room | Two wings address different edges while enclosing two sides of a garden; a wall, canopy or landscape edge can define a third. Useful for a corner, a public/quiet split or a garden open to a selected aspect. | One street may become a blank flank. The inside corner can trap circulation or put two room doors into one small junction. The garden opening also admits exposure, sound and public view. |
| Complete a perimeter court | Put rooms around an open center, separating the public outer edge from an interior outdoor focus. Useful when several groups share supervised external space. | A deep ring consumes the site; corners may lose usable frontage. A court needs a real entrance and service/maintenance route. Its height, width, orientation and nearby obstructions determine sky access; enclosure alone proves no comfort. |
| Leave a passage or view gap | Split or notch the mass to connect destinations, reveal landscape or preserve a sightline. Useful where permeability is a brief priority. | A passage can expose private rooms, become a shortcut through controlled territory or channel wind. Identify opening hours, supervision, ownership and the point where public movement stops. A view gap need not also be a public route. |
| Place pavilions in a landscape | Small parts address different aspects, existing trees or level bands; paths and shared open spaces organize the whole. Useful for independent groups or phased occupation. | Dispersal lengthens everyday and service journeys, increases exposed perimeter and can leave unclaimed gaps. Locate one recognizable arrival and make each landscape interval purposeful. Phasing needs complete routes and useful edges at every stage. |

Compare alternatives using the same program and area boundary. A taller compact
form and a lower spread form can contain the same floor area while producing
different ground coverage, street enclosure, roof area and vertical travel. Do
not substitute an area ratio for a form decision or assume that unused ground is
usable public space.

## Compose entrance, threshold and outdoor room

The entrance is a sequence of spaces: approach → place to pause → threshold →
orientation → destination. Each has a spatial job. A recess creates depth and a
pause off the main path; a projecting canopy announces a door and shelters an
approach; an arcade makes a repeated sheltered edge; a passage compresses space
before a court or hall is revealed. Choose according to actual arrival, waiting,
weather and security needs. A deep recess can hide the door from an oblique
approach, a canopy can drain onto the waiting area, and an arcade can obstruct
shopfront visibility or become an unowned circulation strip.

Place the door where the route is understandable from the real approach, then
test its open position, queuing and the first decision inside. An axial entry
can reveal the main destination immediately; an offset entry can protect privacy
and stage a reveal but needs a visible cue at the turn. Public/private changes
can be expressed by narrowing, a porch, a lobby, material change or a controlled
door. These cues supplement usable geometry and access arrangements. They do
not themselves establish security or an accessible route. Compose the inclusive
journey as a principal experience; check its actual levels and requirements with
[performance and coordination](../../space/circulation/inclusive-and-emergency-interfaces.md) and requirements.

Design an outdoor room by giving it an activity, an occupied edge, access and a
degree of enclosure. A court can center shared life; a terrace can extend one
room; a porch mediates indoor/outdoor use; a garden strip can provide private
outlook without public access. Put seating where people can see an activity or
destination, and provide a choice between exposed and sheltered positions where
the brief warrants it. Locate circulation beside or through the space
deliberately: a path through the middle can divide a gathering area; routing
around it takes extra area and may weaken its role as a meeting place.

Check outdoor use in section as well as plan. The same footprint can feel like
an open clearing with low edges or an enclosed slot with tall ones. Widening the
void, lowering a bounding wing, opening one end or moving the principal occupied
edge changes enclosure, sky view and privacy together. These are alternatives
for assessment, not guaranteed sun, wind or acoustic outcomes. Planting is a
changing spatial edge with establishment, maintenance and seasonal conditions;
it cannot silently replace a required physical barrier.

## Let topography and servicing shape the section

| Ground strategy | Architectural mechanism | What can defeat it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Follow a contour band | A long floor follows a similar ground level; room wings or paths branch toward different aspects. | The route may become long; the uphill face can become buried while the downhill face is exposed. Check ground against each occupied edge and entry. |
| Step discrete parts with the slope | Different program groups occupy different levels, with short links between them; lower roofs may become terraces. | Every step creates access and structural/enclosure interfaces. A sequence of stairs is not a complete inclusive journey. Count all level changes between frequently related activities. |
| Make one platform | Consolidate the main public rooms on a common level; a plinth negotiates changing exterior grade. | Cut/fill, retaining, ramps, drainage and a tall downhill base can dominate the proposal. Compare the full ground section, not only the level plan. |
| Bridge or lift a part | Keep ground or a route continuous below an occupied part. | The covered ground may be dark or hard to supervise; support, exposed underside, vertical access and maintenance become major decisions. Reserve them before presenting the void as available landscape. |

Choose the public entry level from the arrival route and main activity, then
locate secondary levels and landscape. Draw a conceptual section through road,
threshold, occupied floor, uphill ground and downhill edge. Record what is
assumed about surveyed levels, ground conditions, water movement and neighboring
levels. Geological stability, retaining design, waterproofing and drainage
capacity need specialist evidence; architectural intent does not resolve them.

A service edge needs room to work, not just an inconspicuous door. Connect
delivery arrival, unloading/holding, storage, waste and the uses served; reserve
equipment replacement and maintenance approaches where relevant. A rear lane
may separate goods from public arrival, but a service route that crosses a
garden can destroy its claimed quiet use. On a slope, separate public and service
levels can reduce crossings yet introduce goods lifts or internal level changes.
Compare that continuing operational burden with a shared-level alternative.
When only one edge is available, spatial separation, a screened holding area or
agreed time separation are different strategies; do not assume scheduling will
be honored without an operating decision.

Return to the parti when resolving edge, entry or level conflicts changes the
central relationship. Carry the selected site logic into
[mass, section and facade decisions](../../composition/index.md), and record the
survey, consultation or operating input that could reverse it.

- Requires — [Ground and neighbor dependencies](../../construction/structure/ground-and-basement.md#map-neighboring-ground-and-construction-dependencies) when the proposed terrain cut or below-ground use touches neighboring support, retained ground or a construction sequence; a site section is not geotechnical evidence.
- Verified by — [Complete access and closure](../../space/circulation/access-and-closure.md) when arrival or service levels change: follow the actual person, load and support destinations in each operating state, rather than relying on separated arrows.
