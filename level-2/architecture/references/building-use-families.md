# Building use families

Use these bounded methods when a use label hides the activities, support or
operating boundaries that will determine the building. The perimeter is housing
and shared domestic amenities, ordinary learning clusters, public libraries and
short-stay accommodation, including interfaces between them. It does not cover
every building type, specialist laboratory, childcare facility, archive, hospital,
commercial kitchen or hotel classification.

General [demand and sharing](program-and-space.md#what-activities-and-capacity-must-the-program-accommodate),
[area accounting](program-and-space.md#how-can-areas-be-counted-once-and-compared),
[room construction](planning-and-section.md#construct-a-room-that-can-be-used)
and [access zones](program-and-space.md#how-do-people-and-goods-move-through-access-zones)
keep their existing owners. Here, decide **which operational relationships each
family adds**, then use those methods to fit and count them. A family is a design
hypothesis, not a regulatory classification or an instruction to copy a plan.

## Select a family by its operating commitments

Start with the operator's actual commitments: who has a continuing right to use
the space, who arrives without an appointment, what is issued or stored, who
resets a setting, and what must remain available when another use closes. Record
uncertain commitments as alternative scenarios. A bedroom shared by booked
guests and a permanent household's bedroom can look similar while requiring
different arrival, storage, privacy and servicing arrangements.

For the chosen family, develop one representative **activity plus its support**
before repeating the main room. Include the exceptional case that stresses the
scheme: a teaching delivery during classes, a returned-book trolley while an
event begins, a resident entering during public closing, or a guest room being
cleaned beside an occupied room. Locate where objects wait between operations;
an arrow between two rooms cannot hold them. Identify who controls each transfer.

Test at least two spatial responses when the relationship controls the plan:
distributed support versus a central base; repeated corridor versus local
cluster; one public level versus stacked departments; or independent addresses
versus a shared reception. Compare the cost in staffed points, duplicated stock,
occupied frontage, carrying/vertical travel and loss of privacy. Use actual
operating evidence to choose; there is no universal best family.

## Housing and shared domestic support

**Decision.** Decide which domestic activities belong inside each home and which
can credibly be shared, without replacing a required private activity with an
unavailable communal promise. The coupled dwelling/access and furnished-plan
methods remain in [housing](housing-and-court.md#choose-a-dwelling-and-access-family-together)
and [dwelling fit](dwelling-fit.md#test-a-dwelling-through-its-actual-use).

1. Identify household-held objects and routines: everyday food, clothes, work,
   personal storage, visitors and any essential activity required on one level.
   Keep these attached to the household unless the brief explicitly supports
   sharing. A shared lounge does not establish an adequate private living setting.
2. For a proposed shared laundry, guest room, workroom or gathering space, name
   the users, booking/access arrangement, cleaning responsibility and objects
   left there. Follow an ordinary resident carrying laundry or bringing a guest
   from the home door to the room. Include waiting, unattended belongings and
   return after the advertised public opening hours.
3. Compare a local shared room beside a landing with a ground-level common room.
   The local room can reduce carrying and serve a recognizable group, but puts
   arrivals and repeated activity near homes and duplicates support. The ground
   room can connect to a court and consolidate servicing, but adds vertical
   travel and may attract a wider public. Neither location decides ownership.
4. Put public or bookable activity at a deliberate threshold to the residential
   route. A shared lift lobby is a consequential meeting of strangers, residents,
   guests and deliveries; test views into homes, closing and maintenance access.
   A buffer room needs a usable entrance and operational purpose; a label alone
   does not provide privacy or acoustic separation.

**Critical condition.** A resident must not lose access to their home, required
support or agreed amenity when a separately operated public room closes. Show
where visitors stop and where household-held items remain secure. If sharing
depends on bookings or staff, state what happens when the room is occupied or
unstaffed. Do not infer household size, statutory dwelling area or accessibility
from the family name.

**Small counterexample.** Moving all household work desks into a bookable room
below the homes appears to free bedroom frontage. It fails a brief requiring
simultaneous private evening work when that room is already booked for a public
class. Retain the required work setting inside each affected home, or seek an
explicit brief change and additional independently available settings. The
communal room remains a supplement until that conflict is resolved.

## Learning clusters and preparation

**Decision.** Organize ordinary classrooms, group work and nonhazardous practical
learning around how teaching materials and people actually change settings.
Confirm age/user group, supervision model, class organization and whether the
room is assigned to one group or booked by several. Specialist experiments,
machines, substances and safeguarding requirements need their own evidence.

1. For each repeated setting distinguish explanation/demonstration, individual
   or group activity, and preparation/clearing. Identify the instructor's position,
   display, materials, bags/coats and any work kept between sessions. A room used
   for a wet or bulky project cannot be treated as an empty seminar room at the
   next booking without somewhere to put the work and equipment.
2. Locate the preparation and storage interface. A preparation room between two
   teaching rooms can serve both directly, but the same person, bench or stock
   cannot be available to simultaneous incompatible tasks. A central store
   consolidates inventory and delivery, while local stores reduce carrying and
   interruptions. Trace the actual cart or carried load from receiving through
   storage to the teaching setting; do not route it across seated learners by
   default. Check whether staff can prepare while a class remains in use.
3. Compare a corridor of independently entered rooms with a cluster around a
   shared group-work setting. The corridor supports independent classes; the
   cluster can make group work and supervision nearby. In the cluster, give every
   simultaneously used enclosed room an access path that does not cross another
   lesson. Decide whether the central space is teaching, break activity or
   through movement at each period; its area cannot silently serve all three.
4. Translate this into frontage and section. Perimeter classrooms need actual
   occupied exposure and usable display walls; extra doors/windows reduce those
   walls. A central preparation band can release frontage, but needs its service
   access and environmental strategy. A noisy practical room beside or above a
   quiet class needs an evaluated separation, not just a different room name.
   Ground-level practical rooms may simplify large deliveries and outdoor work;
   upper placement can preserve scarce public frontage but adds vertical handling.
5. Test the break and changeover: one group leaving, another arriving, stored work
   remaining, staff resetting and toilets still reachable. If a classroom or
   cluster opens for evening community use, include its own reachable support
   and a complete boundary to the rest of the teaching area.

**Critical condition.** An open learning cluster is conditional on the teaching
and supervision model; it does not prove acoustic compatibility. A shared
preparation room is a support resource with capacity and access limits, rather
than free residual area. Required sightlines, acoustics, daylight and applicable
education/access/fire provisions remain separate checks.

**Small choice.** Two simultaneously occupied teaching rooms share preparation
support, but only one has a door from the common route; the second is reached
through the first.
Keep the shared preparation connection if useful, and add independent access
from the cluster route to the second class. If the fixed display wall prevents
that door, compare relocating the shared room with changing the teaching model;
renaming the first class a circulation space does not repair the original brief.

## Libraries as reader and collection systems

**Decision.** Choose how readers find and use material and how that material
returns to availability. Confirm open-access versus staff-fetched stock,
collection formats and growth/discard policy, individual versus social use,
staffing and independent event hours. Seat count alone does not define a library.

1. Follow a first-time visitor from entry/help to browsing, reading or a group
   room and departure. Locate information and issue/return functions at a
   meaningful choice or controlled interface. A desk must support actual staff
   work and views; a centrally drawn symbol does not establish supervision of
   tall shelves, remote rooms or another floor. State any staffing assumption.
2. Follow a returned or new item through receipt, temporary holding, checking or
   processing, sorting and reshelving. Reserve the carts, work surfaces and
   waiting stock while those stages occur. For staff-fetched material, also trace
   request → retrieval → handover → return. Keep public queues and quiet seats
   out of that working circuit. Test where deliveries and discarded/repair items
   go; a return slot without accessible holding is an incomplete operation.
3. Distinguish browsing stock from quiet work and conversational/group activity.
   Compare a legible open field with quiet enclosed rooms against separated
   departments around a common route. The open field can simplify orientation
   and flexible browsing, but carries speech and interruptions. Departmental
   separation can protect quieter use while increasing thresholds, duplicated
   help points or staff travel. Do not make the quiet reading zone the passage
   to a group/event room or another department.
4. Fit collection capacity from the actual shelf system, usable shelf lengths,
   formats, accessible reach and a stated occupation/growth assumption. Keep
   shelf access, browsing, reader seating and cart movement distinct. More books
   cannot be added by filling a main cross-route or removing all sorting space.
   Mobile/high-density stock changes retrieval, loading and environmental needs;
   it is a separately coordinated option, not spare capacity in an ordinary room.
5. Compare one main public level with stacked departments. A broad level may
   reduce reader/staff vertical trips and simplify oversight, but takes site
   frontage and may leave a deep interior. Stacking can separate noisy and quiet
   uses or fit a constrained site, but needs an intelligible reader route and
   a workable stock/cart connection through every level. A tall atrium joins
   views and can also join sound; a quiet upper gallery is an unproven hypothesis.
   Place sustained reading with deliberate exterior/light relationships and
   protect collections from unsuitable exposure; establish performance separately.
6. Make an independently used event/group room and its support reachable when
   collection access closes. Decide which desk, lobby, toilet, cloak/storage and
   staff facilities remain available. Decide whether returned items can still be
   received and who processes them next; independent events do not imply an
   unattended open collection.

**Critical condition.** Collection movement consumes real floor and staff time.
Changing shelving, stack height or stock density changes structural and access
questions; no load value is adopted here. Rare collections, preservation archives,
security systems and specialist environmental control exceed this public-library
method. A spatially controlled boundary is not a verified theft/fire strategy.

**Small stock/support choice.** In an original trial, six double-faced shelf bays
each have four usable shelves per face and 0.90 m usable length per shelf:
6 × 2 × 4 × 0.90 = **43.20 linear m**. These are hypothetical inventory inputs,
not a product or recommended spacing. A stated collection occupies 38 linear m;
returned items from that same collection are already included in the 38, rather
than new stock. Removing one bay to make a sorting pocket leaves **36 linear m**,
so the apparently helpful support repair loses 2 linear m of required storage
even before any growth/working reserve. Compare keeping six bays and reducing
an optional seating setting, or securing another staff-side sorting location.
Choose only after fitting the pocket, carts and retained seating, and agreeing
any change to seat capacity. This arithmetic checks shelf length; it does not
prove accessible reach, shelf loading, plan fit or a sufficient collection reserve.

## Short-stay rooms and recurring service

**Decision.** Organize temporary occupation around both guest experience and the
operator's repeated reset of rooms. Confirm stay pattern, guests per room, room
mix, staffing, arrival/departure overlap, luggage, linen/cleaning arrangements
and what food, meeting or leisure services are actually included.

1. Distinguish public arrival/reception, controlled guest access, private room,
   staff base and service/storage. Trace a new guest with luggage to a particular
   door while another guest leaves and staff service an occupied floor. Test
   privacy at the door and whether waiting bags occupy the onward route.
2. Derive support from turnover: clean linen and consumables arriving; cart
   preparation; room cleaning/reset; soiled linen/waste held before removal;
   equipment parked and cleaned. State whether linen is processed on site or
   collected externally. These choices change rooms and delivery frequencies.
   Keep clean supply and used material deliberately organized; a closet labeled
   housekeeping does not prove the quantities or simultaneous operations fit.
3. Compare a central service base with local floor bases. Central stock reduces
   duplication but adds repeated vertical/longitudinal carrying and dependence
   on its route. Local bases shorten the last service leg but consume room bays,
   require replenishment and put work nearer guest doors. A separate service
   route can reduce guest encounters but needs area and a functioning connection;
   a shared route is conditional on real cart passing/parking and operating times.
4. Compare a compact repeated room wing with guest wings around a court or public
   base. The compact wing can consolidate circulation and wet/support interfaces,
   but ordinary rooms may have only one exterior aspect. A court or split-wing
   arrangement can give orientation and local identity while lengthening service
   rounds and exposing rooms to public activity. A large public base below
   repeated rooms must reconcile different bays, occupied heights, service paths
   and acoustic interfaces in section; stacking room outlines does not do this.
5. Fit representative room types for sleeping, luggage/storage, work/sitting and
   bathroom use, including entry privacy and actual cleaning access. Include an
   end/corner and any differently accessed room before repeating the bay. Do not
   equate a guest room with a complete dwelling: longer stays or self-catering
   commitments may add food, clothes and personal-stock needs that alter the
   program, without deciding its legal classification.

**Critical condition.** Guest/public and service operations may overlap when
room turnover is greatest. If sharing one lift or corridor is proposed, test that
overlap and a delayed collection; a timetable unsupported by the operator is
not separation. Local requirements and specialist evidence own room standards,
classification, food hygiene, fire/access, acoustic and engineering conclusions.

## Combine families at an explicit interface

Resolve each use on its own operating basis, then decide what it exchanges with
its neighbor. Sharing a room, arrival space, staff position, toilet, store, lift
or court are different decisions. For each proposed shared resource, record:
the users and hours; who holds keys/stock and resets it; where displaced objects
go; the boundary when one use closes; and the consequence of a missed reset or
delivery. Preserve a viable ordinary day for each operator, including residents.

Compare an independent street address and support chain with a common arrival
and controlled branches. The independent option can simplify hours and identity
but duplicates occupied/support space and takes frontage. The common option can
support shared help and amenities, but creates queues, control and staffing
dependencies. If it requires a staffed desk whenever either use operates, put
that commitment in the comparison rather than declaring the shared lobby cheaper.

Develop a section through the consequential interface: noisy evening group room
under homes, book stock above public space, short-stay rooms beside an event
volume, or a teaching store replenished from another level. Mark the actual
occupied floors, route/handling connection, service depth and boundary that must
continue. Use [levels and cores](planning-and-section.md#organize-levels-cores-and-service-depth)
for the general construction and [performance](performance-and-coordination.md)
for physical paths. Neither a different floor nor a commercial label proves
compatibility. The combination may need a changed room assignment, a genuine
buffer/separation, reduced simultaneous use or independent support.

## Worked mixed-use correction

This original hypothetical study concerns only two public rooms and their
interface with homes above. It is not a complete building or an area standard.
The accepted brief requires a small library open until 18:00, a classroom for
evening groups, and uninterrupted residential entry. Books are returned during
the evening; one worker processes them the following morning. A side lane can
receive deliveries. Keep the existing residential core, public frontage and
classroom capacity fixed. Available rooms and their dimensions remain project
inputs to be fitted with the linked room method.

**Faulty proposal.** The shared street foyer opens into the reading room, which
is the sole path to the classroom and public toilet. The book-return trolley
stands beside that path. The residential core also opens from the reading room.
At 18:00 the library is shown closed by locking the foyer-to-reading door. This
blocks the class, toilet and home journeys; leaving it unlocked exposes the
collection and makes quiet seats the evening passage. Calling the reading room
an adaptable foyer deletes its promised reading activity without permission.

**A — shared foyer, independent branches.** Take an edge strip from the reading
room reservation to form a public passage from foyer to classroom and toilet.
Give the reading room a continuous closable edge to that passage, and connect
the existing residential core directly to the foyer through its own controlled
threshold. Move return holding and morning sorting behind a staff-side interface
connected to the side-lane receiving route; an evening return opening, if used,
feeds that holding without opening a public path into it. Check item sizes,
cart approach and the operator's collection procedure before choosing equipment.
This costs reading-room depth/frontage and requires refitting shelves and seats.
If the required reading capacity no longer fits, A fails despite its better routes.

**B — independent evening entrance.** Keep the reading-room boundary and give
the classroom and toilet an independent external approach. Retain a separately
controlled residential branch from the street foyer and the staff-side return
holding. B preserves more reading space but consumes external edge, can lengthen
the evening approach and duplicates arrival/control functions. It remains a
candidate only if the fixed frontage, inclusive journey and actual door locations
support it; an entrance arrow is not enough.

**Decision and verification.** Prefer A only if the room refit retains required
reading and teaching capacity and the shared arrival works without an unprovided
evening attendant. At 17:55 trace readers leaving, class users arriving, a
resident entering and a returned item reaching holding. At 18:05 repeat with
the collection closed, then follow next morning's trolley from holding through
sorting to shelves without crossing an occupied class. Inspect the section for
evening activity below bedrooms and the carrying route through the existing
core; retain acoustic and other performance questions explicitly. If either
candidate needs changed capacity or frontage beyond the boundary, report the
specific conflict and decision required. Route repair alone is not acceptance.

## Source and applicability boundary

The source map in [sources](sources.md#architecture-library-selected-content)
records the identified scans, actual read pages and adoption limits. Selected
Neufert hotel, school and library pages inform the activity/support relationships
and spatial alternatives. Housing uses the established local dwelling/access
methods; shared-amenity and mixed-use tests are original design synthesis. No
published plan, image, table or benchmark is reproduced. The worked corrections
are original hypothetical decisions, not source building analyses.

The Russian-language 2011 Neufert volume is a translation of a German reference,
not evidence that its DIN, regional rules, hotel stars, equipment examples,
numeric dimensions, floor loads or environmental prescriptions apply locally.
Use [requirements](requirements.md) and the selected jurisdiction module for
current applicability. Actual users, collections, equipment, operators, site,
structure and services can change a family choice. These methods establish
bounded architectural reasoning, not complete competence in every listed use.
