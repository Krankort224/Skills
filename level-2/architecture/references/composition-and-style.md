# Composition and style

Use this reference to make and compare architectural choices, not to infer a
style or a regulation from a name. Begin with the brief and existing decisions
in [program and space](program-and-space.md). Check envelope and environmental
consequences through [performance and coordination](performance-and-coordination.md)
and source applicability through [requirements](requirements.md).

## How should the design respond to site and arrival?

**Decision:** Which existing conditions should the proposal continue, contrast,
protect or reinterpret, and how should a person reach and understand the entry?
This is a reading of a particular site, not proof of legal rights, physical
performance or community preference.

**Inputs:** Site boundary and orientation, levels, known access and neighboring
uses, public routes, views, existing fabric and landscape, exposure and the
program's public/private relationships. Identify the source and confidence of
each; if any are missing, proceed with labeled alternatives rather than inventing
site facts. Establish applicable restrictions separately through requirements.

1. Trace the site edges in plan: identify active and quiet frontages, access
   points, service approaches, neighboring openings and possible conflicts.
   Distinguish observed conditions from presumed future use.
2. Follow an arrival sequence from the public approach to the threshold and the
   first destination. At each turn ask what is visible, where a person waits,
   whether the entrance reads clearly and where public access changes to private.
   Include the accessible route and service arrival as distinct routes to check,
   not an assumption that the prominent entrance serves everyone.
3. Locate view origins and targets, landform, drainage patterns, vegetation,
   areas of exposure and sheltered places where known. Mark a valued view both
   from the site and from surrounding public space; one can conflict with the
   other. Do not assume a view is legally protected without a verified source.
4. Select the consequential cues: for example align an entry with an existing
   path, retain a tree as an outdoor focus, or contrast a quiet facade with a
   busy edge. State why each cue matters to the program and who benefits.
5. Compare an alternative response where cues compete. A continuous street edge
   may define arrival but obscure a garden; a gap may reveal it while weakening
   enclosure or exposing the entrance. Check access, privacy, maintenance and
   exposure before selecting. Identify surveys or consultation needed to resolve
   unknown conditions.

For mechanisms, suitability and failure modes of street walls, forecourts,
courts, passages, pavilions, thresholds and sloping-ground strategies, use
[site and context](site-and-context.md). Carry its selected edge, arrival and
level relationships into the parti rather than treating site reading as a
separate descriptive exercise.

**Check:** A coherent response can be described as a sequence and a set of
relationships without a required drawing. If a site plan is requested, label
orientation, sources and uncertainties. Do not represent unverified restrictions
or environmental effects as established facts.

## How can different partis be generated and compared?

**Decision:** Choose an organizing relationship between program, movement,
open space and site. A parti is a working proposition, not a typology assigned
by building name or a demand that every later decision follow one diagram.

**Inputs:** Main activities, required adjacencies and separations, entrances,
relative areas, site reading, fixed constraints and priorities. Separate unknown
areas from confirmed ones and use the same definitions across options.

1. State the central design tension in ordinary language, such as a shared
   public destination versus quiet rooms. Choose two or more materially different
   organizing moves: a spine linking rooms, a court surrounded by rooms, or
   clusters connected through shared space. Change the relationship, not merely
   the facade or color.
2. For each option place the primary destination, entry, main route and major
   groups. Describe how a person reaches them and how the proposal meets the
   site. Note which connections are essential and which could change.
3. Test each against the same program: critical adjacencies and separations,
   public/private transitions, room fit, arrival, external space and potential
   expansion. Flag unresolved access, area and structural/service interfaces.
   An attractive organizing idea does not establish that the rooms fit.
4. First identify conflicts with verified hard constraints. Do not average a
   failure away with aesthetic preferences. Then compare remaining benefits,
   disadvantages, assumptions and consequences; use project-specific priorities
   if ranking is useful. Explain uncertainty rather than inventing scores.
5. Select a direction with its reason and the issue that could reverse it. A
   hybrid is useful only if its combined routes, areas and interfaces are retested;
   adding a court to a spine is not automatically an improvement.

### Organizational types are working hypotheses

A type describes relationships, not a building category. Start from the brief's
most consequential tension and choose a mechanism that could resolve it. For
example, frequent exchange between groups suggests a shared center; independent
opening hours suggest separable wings and a common threshold; repeated private
rooms suggest a repeatable band. A school, library or house can use several of
these mechanisms. Program names alone cannot select one.

| Organization | Spatial mechanism and useful conditions | Conflicts, failures and a discriminating test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Linear spine / bar | Rooms or groups repeat along a route; one continuous direction makes a sequence legible and can follow a site edge or contour. The bar is the volume; the spine is the movement relationship and need not occupy its center. Useful for repeated rooms, staged visits or a dominant long edge. | An end entrance puts the farthest use at the end of every journey. A central entrance shortens some journeys but interrupts a sequence. Single-loaded access gives rooms one side and the route the other; double-loaded access packs rooms on both sides but removes some external frontage. Test actual route-to-room depth and whether important journeys pass through another activity. |
| Court / perimeter | Occupied bands define an open center; shared doors, galleries and outlook make it an outdoor focus. Outer and inner edges can carry different degrees of publicness. Useful when a common external space and a defined boundary are both central. | A court consumes footprint; a ring corridor and room band can make it too small. Inside corners may be circulation junctions, deep rooms or lost area. Test the remaining void after bands, route and corner uses are drawn; check plan and section from its users' positions. |
| Central hall / hub with wings | A common destination or distribution space sits between distinct groups; radial or branching wings extend toward different aspects. Useful when all groups need a shared place or frequent contact with a common resource. | The center can become a queue, noisy crossroads or unavoidable controlled zone. Wedges between wings may not fit rectangular rooms. Test simultaneous arrivals, a closed wing and movement between two outer groups; a hub is useful only if it can serve that demand. |
| Cluster / campus | Semi-independent parts form local identities around shared landscape or small commons; links connect the groups. Useful for different schedules, topographic bands, phased growth or autonomy. | Many entrances weaken orientation; frequent cross-cluster trips multiply distance and exposed travel. Expansion can consume the very commons that organizes the first phase. Test one ordinary user's full day and one complete phase, including service routes and support, not just a final site diagram. |
| Grid / mat | A repeated field admits different room combinations; courts, wider routes or missing bays create hierarchy. A mat combines occupied cells and voids into a low continuous fabric. Useful for repeated activities and possible regrouping. | A grid does not make every use interchangeable: furniture, support, services and external frontage constrain cells. Undifferentiated routes are hard to navigate; infilling a court can make adjacent rooms deep. Test a large room, a small room group, a principal route and a future change within the same order. |
| Stacked plates / podium with narrower upper parts | A broad public or shared base supports smaller repeated upper groups; vertical access separates territory and operating hours. Useful for limited ground or different program sizes. | Stacking separates activities that need direct contact; support positions and service paths may conflict across plates. The roof of a lower part becomes an interface, not free outdoor area. Test entry control, vertical journeys, plausible support continuity and terrace access before treating a shift in volume as beneficial. |
| Atrium / sectional void | Floors and routes share a visible internal center; the void can orient people across levels and distinguish the main destination. Useful when a common interior focus matters more than uninterrupted floor area. | The opening removes usable area and couples sound, privacy and environmental zones. Bridges may become detours; a large glazed roof introduces enclosure, access and fire interfaces. Test the remaining floor bands and sightlines in section; an atrium is not an automatic daylight or ventilation solution. |

Hybrids resolve different demands in different parts: a public hall can head a
quiet linear wing; clusters can share a spine; a regular field can be organized
by a court. Design their junction as a real place with ownership, capacity and
access. A hybrid that keeps each type's drawbacks without its principal benefit
is a reason to simplify, not to add another organizing device.

**Worked comparison:** A small community center needs a visible shared hall,
quiet rooms and an outdoor gathering place. A spine makes the hall easy to find
and separates quiet rooms along a route, but a long route can occupy useful
space. A court puts the rooms around a shared outdoor focus, but its corners and
entry may complicate circulation and the court's exposure is not yet known.
If a direct public route is the overriding brief priority, provisionally choose
the spine; if a protected shared outdoor room is essential and site evidence
supports it, investigate the court. Both still need room-fit and access checks.

## How should mass and void organize the site and rooms?

**Decision:** Distribute occupied parts, outdoor voids and building edges to
support the chosen parti and site relationships. Study these in plan and, where
needed, conceptual section or elevation; this is architectural composition,
not a building model, structural design or verified environmental analysis.

**Inputs:** Site reading, program areas and adjacencies, plausible heights or
levels where known, verified site constraints and the parti alternatives.

1. Start from the major program group and add other parts in their required
   relationships; alternatively start from a provisional available site area
   and remove space for entry, outdoor use or a valued view. The latter is not a
   legally buildable envelope unless all relevant constraints have been verified.
2. Examine the resulting solids and voids together. Does the outdoor space
   have a purpose, access, defined edges and a relationship to its users, or is
   it left over? Does splitting the footprint create useful connections or an
   exposed, difficult-to-maintain gap?
3. Compare one compact arrangement with a more articulated one at the same
   program assumptions. A compact form may reduce exposed perimeter and simplify
   routes but produce deeper rooms; separated wings may offer more external edges
   and identity but lengthen travel and increase exposed junctions. Neither
   implies a predicted energy or daylight result.
4. At the public edge, compare aligning with a neighboring datum, stepping back,
   or creating a gap. At a slope, compare adapting the footprint to contours
   with consolidating levels; mark access and earthwork implications for later
   verification. For views, locate observer and target in plan and section,
   then compare retaining a gap, framing a view or accepting an obstruction.
5. Test the chosen footprint with representative rooms, circulation, openings,
   support and service zones. Note awkward corners, deep interiors, long routes,
   complicated roof or drainage junctions, and interfaces requiring specialist
   review. Return to the parti if these changes undermine its purpose.

### What the massing operations change

**Add volumes** when distinct activities need different heights, orientations or
operating identities. Begin with a critical room or group, add its neighbors and
make their links explicit. The joints can become entries, small courts or service
strips; narrow leftovers without access are failure conditions. Too many separate
roofs and links can weaken the overall order and consume area.

**Carve a volume** when an outer edge, overall compactness or retained fabric
should remain continuous. Subtract a court, entry recess, light slot or terrace
with an identified use. Refit the program after each subtraction: removed area
does not disappear from the brief. A continuous outer shell can conceal awkward
interior depths, so test room bands on both sides of each void.

**Step, shift or taper** when different levels should address different edges,
when a lower datum should relate to neighbors, or when upper mass must withdraw
from a view. These moves change floor area, support alignment, circulation and
roof junctions together. A setback useful in elevation may leave an inaccessible
roof strip; a shifted plate may require an unresolved transfer. Prefer a simpler
alignment when the offset has no spatial benefit.

**Split a mass** when a passage, view or useful outdoor room is more valuable
than continuity. Locate the observer or route and check the gap in section as
well as plan. A slit between tall walls can read as a void in a diagram while
being poor usable ground. Separate parts also need complete arrival, support
and servicing; a glazed link is not area-free.

Compare the *distribution* of mass, not only its total: one tall edge can define
a court and another low edge can open it to sky and neighbors. A high hall beside
lower rooms can make the shared destination legible; equal-height fragments may
make that hierarchy disappear. No universal solid/void percentage establishes
civic character or comfort. Define whether a ratio concerns a site footprint,
section, floor area or facade before using it.

**Worked comparison:** On a corner site, a single compact block can address
both streets with one entrance at the corner, while an L-shaped arrangement can
form a garden behind it. The L gives the garden more enclosure but introduces an
inside corner and may make the second street edge less active. Select only after
checking room access, garden use and the actual site constraints.

For daylight, solar exposure, wind, acoustic and envelope questions, formulate
the architectural alternatives and use [performance and coordination](performance-and-coordination.md).
No geometry alone proves comfort, code compliance or system capacity.

## How can section generate form?

A section is an organizing proposition about ground, occupied height, level
changes, light openings, support and roof. Establish a representative transverse
section through room/route/edge and a longitudinal section through arrival and
the principal destination. These can be described textually when no drawing is
requested. Work with program clearances and provisional architectural zones;
do not derive engineering sizes from visual proportions.

| Section strategy | How it works; when useful | Conflicts and tests |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Common level, varied roof | Put frequent adjacencies on one accessible floor; raise the main hall or selected roof bays above lower support rooms. Useful for shared public use and different room heights. | A tall roof everywhere wastes volume and can obscure hierarchy; a low service band can block the high room's side openings. Test the actual occupied height, roof/support zone and light path across both parts. |
| Room beside a tall void | Stack small rooms beside a double-height destination or atrium; galleries allow visual orientation across levels. Useful when shared identity and oversight are desired. | Visible connection is not acoustic separation. Check privacy, floor-edge safety, routes and the floor area displaced by the void. Return to stacking or adjacency if a quiet use cannot tolerate the shared volume. |
| Stepped floors | Follow ground or give neighboring rooms different volumes and outlooks. Useful where a few program groups genuinely benefit from different levels. | Test a continuous inclusive journey and goods movement. Frequent level changes can make closely related rooms operationally remote. Compare fewer consolidated levels before accepting complex links. |
| Terraced upper parts | Recede floors to make external rooms at intermediate levels and reduce the apparent upper bulk from selected viewpoints. | A terrace needs a destination, access, privacy edge and enclosure/drainage coordination. Do not count a lower roof as usable terrace until those conditions are reserved. |
| Roof monitor / clerestory / repeated roof bays | Open a raised roof edge or place openings between repeated high and low roof parts; brings a potential light source to rooms away from external walls. Useful for a large hall with occupied perimeter rooms. | Roof opening location determines what receives light and what may receive glare. Structure, maintenance, water paths and service zones can obstruct it. Trace the opening-to-task line in section, then refer performance claims for assessment. |

Section and plan must generate each other. A tall room changes roof silhouette,
external scale and the elevation's upper openings. A sloping roof can orient the
interior toward a view or a high light opening, but its low edge must still fit
the activity and service zones. A compressed threshold followed by a taller hall
can clarify arrival through contrast without a monumental exterior. The change
must occur along a usable route, not rely on steps or a hidden alternative entry.

At each level trace plausible aligned supports and vertical service reservations
against the room and opening arrangement. If a desired void or offset interrupts
those lines, reserve the interface and identify who must confirm it. Compare an
aligned option; do not claim that expressive form supplies its own structural
solution. Coordinate through [structure and services reservations](performance-and-coordination.md#structure-and-services-reservations).

## How can scale, hierarchy and rhythm support the idea?

**Decision:** Make the important spaces and routes legible while relating the
building's parts to its whole, its neighbors and the people using it.

**Inputs:** Parti, entry sequence, principal rooms, neighboring datums, proposed
room and facade organization, and any known structural or service interfaces.

1. Identify what should be prominent and why: the entrance, a common room, an
   outdoor space or a route. Compare prominence through position, size, contrast,
   depth or light; do not assume that the largest element is always most public.
2. Examine relationships at site, building, room, opening and person scale.
   Compare a long undivided frontage with one broken into legible parts; assess
   whether entrances and ground-level details can be understood on approach.
   Proportional ratios are optional exploratory devices, not requirements.
3. Establish a repeatable order from actual room needs and plausible support:
   regular bays, alternating openings or a gradual change. Place deliberate
   exceptions where an entrance, corner or special room needs one. Symmetry can
   clarify an axis; asymmetry can balance different uses or site edges.
4. Check the order against both sides of the facade and against the plan. A
   repeated opening that crosses a partition, exposes a private room or conflicts
   with support is a conflict to resolve, not a reason to conceal the problem.
   Adjust room grouping, the rhythm or the exception and compare consequences.
5. Review the sequence from arrival to destination. If every element is equally
   emphatic, reduce competing accents; if the main entry disappears, strengthen
   a meaningful cue. Check that the cue remains usable and accessible.

**Check:** Describe the hierarchy and its exceptions in terms of real uses. A
rhythm that looks consistent in elevation still requires room-fit, structural
and service coordination; do not treat it as proof of constructability.

### Derive an elevation from occupied bays

A bay is a repeated interval in the building's order; it may be structural, a
room group or a facade subdivision. Keep these grids distinct until coordinated.
Start with a representative room and its wall: where do furniture, occupied
positions, views, doors and partitions leave usable opening locations? Add the
floor/roof edge and plausible support zones. Repeat that relationship along the
frontage, then identify deliberate exceptions for entry, common room, end and
corner. Elevation and plan are developed together rather than wrapping an
unrelated pattern around the plan.

| Opening order | Spatial and expressive consequences | Conflict to resolve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Punched openings in a solid field | Individual apertures make room/bay increments and wall depth legible; piers, heads and sills can establish a common order. Useful for cellular rooms or a desired sense of enclosure. | Centering every opening on a room can produce erratic exterior spacing; forcing exterior regularity can place windows across partitions. Group rooms or allow a reasoned exception. Deep reveals alter view and potential light and need their own enclosure detail. |
| Grouped openings within a larger bay | Several small rooms or one shared room sit within a common surround or recess. Useful for relating fine room increments to a larger urban frontage. | The surround must allow actual room divisions and support. A visually continuous opening may conceal opaque spandrels or private rooms; state those differences rather than depict uninterrupted transparency. |
| Horizontal band | Connect openings or glazing along a floor; emphasizes continuity and can allow several internal room arrangements. | Partitions still need viable junctions; solid panels may be needed at support, storage or privacy zones. Long bands crossing an entry or corner can erase hierarchy. Coordinate the true clear opening and opaque zones in each bay. |
| Expressed frame with infill | Columns, beams or a secondary facade frame order glazed and opaque fields; primary and secondary scales can remain legible. | A visible facade frame is not necessarily the load-bearing frame. Distinguish actual support from applied expression and check alignment, depth and maintenance at their interface. |
| Screen or layered edge | A permeable outer layer, loggia or deep reveal mediates street, privacy and an inner opening; its depth can form an inhabited threshold. | A screen can obstruct view, cleaning, doors or rescue/access provisions; a shallow decorative layer may promise inhabitable depth it does not provide. Show the usable zone and independent inner enclosure. |

Choose repetition when room needs repeat, alternation when two complementary
uses repeat, and progression where an actual transition in activity, exposure
or approach justifies gradual change. Irregularity can record different rooms;
it still needs an intelligible order. On an asymmetrical elevation, balance a
large void or tall volume with the length, depth or density of another part;
equal visual weight does not require mirrored functions.

Check the corner as a meeting of two inhabited edges. Wrapping one rhythm may
squeeze the last room or leave a very narrow end panel. Options include a solid
corner pier, an opening that belongs to one face, a recessed joint, or a special
corner room expressed on both faces. Choose for actual outlook, privacy and
support. Similarly decide how the repeated order ends at ground, parapet, roof
and existing building; do not hide cumulative dimensional mismatch in a sliver.

For cladding expression, distinguish a **joint** from an **opening** and a
**facade panel** from a **room bay**. A quiet elevation can use few repeated panel
families; special panels can concentrate at entries and corners. Similar-looking
panels may have different edge lengths, diagonals, handedness, grain or coating
orientation and therefore may not be interchangeable. In a manual study compare
regular flat units, faceted segments or curved units, counting special conditions
and extra joints as well as surface area. Flat facets change the silhouette and
reflections; smaller units follow a curve more closely but multiply edges and
fixings. A triangular panel is planar, but triangulation increases joint length
and node variety and does not establish the assembly's feasibility. Do not set
fabrication tolerances, stock sizes or cost savings without supplier evidence.

These are compositional and rationalization principles; no panel model,
algorithm, fabrication schedule or engineering design is implied. Coordinate
movement, fixing, water and layer continuity through the enclosure reference.

## How can a requested style become design decisions?

**Decision:** Translate a style or reference into a coherent set of observable
choices appropriate to the brief and site. Names are shorthand, not prescriptions
for a period, person, place, material or historical survey.

**Inputs:** The user's desired character and references if supplied, site and
program priorities, proposed parti, budget and maintenance constraints where
known. Ask what the requested character means when different interpretations
would change the decision; otherwise state the working interpretation.

1. Describe the desired experience in concrete terms: formal or informal,
   sheltered or open, restrained or richly articulated, continuous or layered.
   Note whose experience and which spaces matter.
2. Translate the character into levers: overall proportion; symmetry or balance;
   entry and spatial sequence; facade depth and opening rhythm; material, color,
   texture and weathering; and detail or joint expression. Select only levers
   that advance the brief; a style label does not require them all.
3. Produce at least two coherent translations where a choice matters. For
   example, a restrained character might use a regular facade with recessed
   openings, or a small number of larger openings arranged asymmetrically. Both
   can be restrained but differ in privacy, rhythm and room flexibility.
4. Compare each against the context and intended use. A deep reveal can express
   solidity and provide some shelter but affects view and light; an exposed joint
   can clarify assembly but adds visible maintenance points. Refer performance
   questions to the relevant coordination method rather than infer outcomes.
5. State the selected combination and what evidence is needed to refine it.
   If a reference is supplied, extract the transferable relationship and test its
   assumptions about climate, scale and use; do not copy its dimensions or claim
   that its success automatically transfers.

**Check:** Replace vague labels with observable decisions. A style direction is
successful only if its spatial and practical consequences remain compatible with
the brief, not merely because the facade resembles a reference.

### Coherent character can be produced by different traits

| Desired direction | Related spatial and formal choices | Tension to keep visible |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Restrained / minimal | Few strong volumes; clear hierarchy; limited opening and joint families; depth or texture supplies variation rather than many competing accents. | Visually suppressed joints still need space for movement and repair. Uniform surfaces can make an entrance or room identity disappear. |
| Formal / ordered | Axial arrival or balanced fronts; repeated bays; clear base/body/roof relationships; emphasis at a meaningful shared destination. | Symmetry can force dissimilar programs into false equivalence or place entry against the actual approach. Use asymmetric internal organization or break the exterior order where necessary. |
| Grounded / tactile | Mass reads through depth, reveals and a legible base; materials and contact surfaces relate to use and weathering; outdoor thresholds connect to ground. | Heavy appearance does not establish load-bearing action. Deep enclosure may oppose desired openness and views; a durable surface can still be unpleasant at human contact. |
| Light / layered | Slim expressed elements, overlapping planes, visible transitions and an intermediate screen or porch; gaps distinguish parts. | Slender appearance requires coordination, not assumed thin structure. Layering can complicate cleaning and turn useful depth into inaccessible cavities. |
| Informal / clustered | Smaller recognizable parts; local outdoor spaces; several scales of gathering; balanced differences rather than a dominant axis. | Apparent informality must preserve a legible public entrance, coherent routes and complete support uses. Many fragments can consume the commons. |
| Context-responsive | Continue relevant grain, datum, material module or threshold type, while changing relationships to suit the current activities. | Literal imitation can carry inappropriate depth, climate assumptions or social boundaries. Explain what relationship transfers and what changes. |

Style should refine a viable spatial proposition and can also challenge it: an
intended intimate character may reveal that a single enormous lobby contradicts
the brief. Revise the space, not only its finish. Do not infer a user's tastes,
culture, jurisdiction or desired authority from a type label.

## How should materials express and support the design?

**Decision:** Select a provisional material expression for each visible or
consequential location, balancing character, use, durability and plausible
construction. Enclosure layers and their continuity are owned by
[performance and coordination](performance-and-coordination.md#climate-exposure-and-envelope-continuity);
a cladding appearance is not a complete wall assembly or engineering design.

**Inputs:** Intended appearance and sensory character, exposure, use and wear,
site climate where known, available budget and supply information, maintenance
capacity, project performance requirements and expected service life if stated.

1. Identify the role and location: structure, protective outer surface, interior
   finish or connection. Decide whether the assembly should read as a continuous
   surface, a set of units or an expressed frame. A visible finish is not
   necessarily load-bearing; describe the intended relationship honestly.
2. Shortlist plausible alternatives for the same role and compare texture,
   color, module, joint pattern, weathering and the way each meets openings and
   corners. Check what happens at human contact and in exposed or high-wear areas.
3. Compare durability under the actual exposure, cleaning and repair access,
   material availability and lead time, installation skills, initial and likely
   replacement costs, and potential for reuse or separation. Where environmental
   impact matters, compare equivalent functions and life-cycle boundaries using
   credible project-specific evidence; a material name alone proves no ranking.
4. Trace a representative junction: support, fixing, movement, water shedding,
   continuity of enclosure and access for replacement. Ask whether pieces can be
   installed in sequence and replaced without dismantling unrelated work. If an
   interface is unresolved, keep the choice provisional and identify the review
   needed. Do not assume a dry fixing is suitable for every assembly.
5. Separate required performance from an intended appearance and from a named
   product. If alternatives are allowed, identify the characteristics they must
   preserve and how they will be evaluated. Coordinate applicable fire, moisture,
   acoustic, thermal and structural requirements with the relevant specialists
   and verified sources; do not carry foreign classifications or numeric values
   into the project as defaults.

**Worked comparison:** For a sheltered public entrance, unit masonry may make
scale and repair of individual units legible, while a large panel can create a
quieter surface with fewer visible joints. The panel may make a damaged area more
costly to replace; masonry introduces more joints and support details. Neither
is selected until exposure, support, availability and maintenance are checked.

Record the preferred direction, rejected alternative, reason, unknowns and next
check in the user's requested form. A sample or mock-up can help resolve a visible
finish when useful; it is not a gate for a textual concept. Review later changes
against the selected parti, room uses and envelope interfaces.

### Material and tectonic vocabulary

A continuous surface suppresses unit scale and emphasizes overall volume; a
coursed wall makes small increments visible; a panel field gives intermediate
scale; an expressed frame distinguishes support from infill. Use these choices
to connect building, room and hand-contact scales. A rough texture may enrich a
near view but make dirt retention or contact uncomfortable. A polished surface
can reflect activity and light but show glare, stains or uneven alignment.
Color, grain, surface treatment and joint shadow must be considered together.

For unit masonry, long horizontal courses can emphasize continuity while grouped
vertical reveals restore room scale. A bond or veneer can refer to masonry's
assembly without proving a load path. For panel cladding, joint placement can
align with openings, floors or deliberate independent subdivisions; document
which is intended. For timber or directional sheet finishes, turning grain at a
corner changes the reading and can create a special unit. For metal, folded
edges, seams and shadow lines can reveal thinness; flatness and finish uniformity
are project/supplier questions, not an automatic property of the material name.

Tectonic expression concerns what support, joining and assembly are made visible.
An exposed post-and-beam order, a concealed frame behind a continuous surface,
and a frame behind a visibly separate screen are legitimate different intentions.
Describe the actual and apparent roles so a massive-looking cladding is not
claimed as bearing, or a decorative fin as structure. Select where a joint should
be expressed, recessed or visually suppressed; all still require movement and
maintenance coordination. Compare a representative opening, corner and base;
a palette that works only on an uninterrupted wall is incomplete.

Treat weathering as part of appearance: runoff can mark a light wall, contact can
polish a rough surface, timber can change color and metals can form patina or
stain adjacent finishes. Desired aging and unacceptable deterioration must be
distinguished using actual exposure, product evidence and maintenance practice.
Different components have different replacement cycles. A long-lived outer
finish surrounding an inaccessible short-lived seal is a coordination concern,
not a reason to promise a maintenance-free facade. Compare equivalent functions
and boundaries before environmental or whole-life cost claims. A sample or
mock-up, when appropriate to stage, should test the relevant combination of
unit, joint, opening and corner rather than color in isolation.

### A corner community building: public presence and a quiet garden

**Problem.** Suppose a level site has a busy north street, an east pedestrian
approach, a west service lane and a desired south garden. The brief asks for a
shared hall, quieter rooms and independent evening hall use. Assume a rectangular
study zone 30 m east–west by 24 m north–south; actual rights and restrictions are
unverified. A candidate occupied footprint of 368 m² is being compared under the
same program, without claiming that this is enough gross area.

**Hypothesis.** An L along the north and west can define the street edge, place
quiet rooms away from the eastern arrival and enclose a southeast outdoor room.
A compact 23 m by 16 m block gives the same 368 m² footprint but does not define
that garden as directly. Compare its shorter internal relationships with the L's
two different external edges; neither form receives an automatic preference.

**Arrangement and test.** Let the L comprise a north band 30 by 8 m and west band
8 by 24 m, overlapping at their corner: 240 + 192 − 64 = 368 m². The unbuilt
southeast rectangle within the study zone is 22 by 16 m. This verifies footprint
and void geometry only. If the hall fills the full depth of the north band, the
route from the eastern entrance to the western quiet wing passes through the
hall: the hall cannot close independently while that route remains open.

**Revision.** Put the north gallery along the garden side, 2 m deep for this
study, and the west gallery along that wing's garden side. Place the hall north
of its gallery, quiet rooms west of theirs and a lobby at the eastern end. The
routes meet in the L's inside corner; hall doors can close while the gallery
continues to the quiet wing. The hall band is now only 6 m deep before any wall,
support or service allowance. Test its actual equipment/seating arrangement;
if that depth fails, deepen the north wing or move the hall to a taller wider
end volume, recounting footprint and garden area. Do not keep the old area total
after enlarging it.

A wider hall volume can make the principal destination legible at the north
elevation, while repeated quiet-room openings give the west wing a finer scale.
A lower gallery roof beside a taller hall offers a clerestory opportunity toward
the garden, contingent on its real light path and support/service zones. Keep the
entry visible from the east and put its waiting place beside the gallery rather
than in the through-route. Test west-lane deliveries to storage without crossing
the garden or passing through an occupied quiet room. If that fails, relocate
support and retest the room count; a hidden door alone is not service separation.

**Remaining inputs.** Actual area schedule and furniture, simultaneous use and
opening hours, land survey, legal envelope, public/goods access, room heights,
noise/exposure, support and service reservations, and enclosure/access/fire
requirements could reverse the choice. The result is an original plan, mass,
section and elevation intention derived from the stated tension, not proof of
feasibility or a selected building model.

### A sloping library: shared reading, quiet work and an ordered facade

**Problem.** Suppose a narrow site falls from an upper street to a lower garden.
The brief wants a shared reading room, small quiet rooms, a legible principal
entrance and delivery access from below. A stepped sequence may follow the
landform, but frequent trips between shared and quiet spaces make a staircase-only
sequence unsuitable. Surveyed levels and program areas are not yet known. For
the section test only, take the upper-street arrival as level 0, the lower garden as −3 m and
local ground against the uphill boundary as +1.2 m; these are invented inputs.

**Hypothesis.** Keep entrance, shared reading and the main quiet-room route on
one common public level. Use a taller reading volume beside a lower band of
small rooms; let a lower service level meet the garden-side service approach.
Compare this with several half-level public rooms: the latter may follow ground
more closely but adds a level transition to every cross-room journey.

**Arrangement and test.** In a provisional transverse section reserve, from
street to garden, a quiet-room band, a continuous route and the taller reading
room. Trace upper-street threshold → orientation point → quiet room or reading
room without a public level change. Then draw ground against that section. A
provisional quiet-room sill at +0.9 m is 0.3 m below the assumed adjacent ground at +1.2 m: that portion of
the opening faces soil, and the plan's claim to unobstructed direct outlook
fails unless the ground/enclosure relationship changes. Moving the whole plate
down would also break the upper-street entry relationship. Service delivery below also needs a real
connection to book/storage demand above; note that vertical movement as an
operating interface rather than assume it is effortless.

**Revision.** Put storage, enclosed support and vertical service connection on
the uphill side; move quiet rooms to a lateral exposed edge and retain the shared
reading room toward the garden. The roof above the reading space rises beyond
the support band, allowing a potential high opening; its orientation and glare
consequences need assessment. If the lateral frontage cannot accommodate the
quiet-room demand, compare a court cut into the uphill mass with an additional
public level. The court removes floor area and adds enclosure/ground interfaces;
the extra level adds vertical journeys. State which brief priority governs that
trade-off before choosing.

For the lateral elevation, suppose four equal 4 m room bays occupy a 16 m
frontage and a candidate panel module is 1 m. Four panels per bay fit that
nominal order. Centering a nominal 1.6 m opening in each bay, however, puts jambs
1.2 m and 2.8 m from its start, off that panel grid. This is a geometric mismatch,
not a defect proved by a style rule. Compare special jamb panels, a smaller
subdivision or a revised opening. If a 2 m centered opening is explored, nominal
jambs fall on the 1 m lines, but the view, desk layout, glazing performance and
support zones must still work. Actual joints, tolerances and wall thicknesses
are unresolved; this arithmetic is not a fabrication schedule.

Let the taller reading room have a larger grouped opening and the entry a deep
recess within the same datum order. This differentiates the shared destination
from quiet bays while maintaining a related base, opening heads and joint
vocabulary. At the corner choose a solid end pier or a separate recessed joint
instead of forcing the module into a narrow sliver. Describe a unit or panel
cladding expression separately from the actual enclosing layers; visible
solidity does not imply a bearing masonry wall.

**Remaining inputs.** Survey and ground/water evidence, actual frontage and
room demand, inclusive route levels, delivery method and operating capacity,
reading light/glare, quiet-room acoustic needs, support/roof/service reservations,
product modules and maintenance access. If any invalidates the common public
level or lateral room band, return to the section hypothesis and recount area;
do not repair the drawing with cosmetic facade offsets.
