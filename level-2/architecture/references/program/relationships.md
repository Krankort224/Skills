# Program relationships

Select this method for its question; reuse accepted project facts and decisions. Numerical examples are hypothetical, not area standards. Apply [requirements](../requirements.md) only to the actual jurisdiction and use; demand does not establish physical fit or compliance.

## Which relationships must the plan satisfy?

**Decision and boundary.** Translate operational relationships into constraints
that a spatial option can actually satisfy. Start at department or zone scale;
resolve internal room relationships separately where useful. A proximity label
alone does not describe the required connection.

1. List the relevant spaces or zones on both axes of an adjacency table, or use
   equivalent pairwise notes. For each significant pair record the reason,
   source and status: essential, desirable, neutral, undesirable or prohibited.
   A prohibition or other hard requirement cannot be traded away by a score.
2. Specify the relationship: direct door or shared boundary; short route; same
   level; visual supervision; acoustic or other separation; controlled access;
   shared service interface; or vertical connection. A visual link can coexist
   with acoustic separation; a controlled connection is not open public access.
   Define any quantitative distance only from project evidence or an applicable
   source, with its route and units. Mark unknowns.
3. Examine each row for missing support links and each pair for conflicts. If A
   needs both B and C close but B and C must be separated, test a buffer, separate
   approaches, a vertical connection or a duplicated service. Compare operational
   cost, staff, distance and access implications; do not silently drop a hard
   separation to obtain a neat cluster.
4. Translate relationships into zones and test a bubble or 2D block arrangement.
   Trace each essential connection and separation, including those across floors.
   The diagram communicates intent; it is not a dimensional or compliance check.
   Validate priorities and compromises with the relevant users and decision maker.

**Small check.** A reception must see both a public waiting area and a restricted
work area, while visitors must not enter the latter. A glazed, controlled boundary
can satisfy visibility and separation; a shared open doorway cannot satisfy both.

### Turn relationships into an organizing scheme

A sequence suits activities whose order matters, such as receiving → preparation
→ use; place a bypass route where others must reach the last room without passing
through the process. A hub suits many destinations needing the same reception or
shared resource; protect the hub from becoming a queue or noisy crossing. A
cluster suits intense local relationships and weaker inter-group movement;
connect clusters through a clear common route so one group does not become a
passage for another. A ring suits repeated access to a shared court or collection;
give it recognizable entries and destinations to avoid a disorienting circuit.

Start by placing fixed connections and the most constrained large room. Then
arrange the compatible rooms around their shared resource, put incompatible uses
on different branches or across a real buffer, and connect clusters to arrival.
Draw doors and movement through the proposed blocks before calling them a plan.
Touching rectangles do not prove a direct connection: the shared wall may be
occupied by storage or the only door may open into the wrong access zone. Compare
[plan families](../space/planning/room-floor-and-section.md#choose-a-plan-family-from-the-relationships)
when the same graph can be realized in different ways.
