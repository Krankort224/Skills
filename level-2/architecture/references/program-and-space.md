# Program and space

Use this reference to develop or revise a traceable spatial proposal. Its methods
are selectable by question, not a required sequence. Reuse accepted project facts
and decisions. Pair it with [requirements](requirements.md),
[composition and style](composition-and-style.md), and
[performance and coordination](performance-and-coordination.md) as needed.

## What activities and capacity must the program accommodate?

**Decision and boundary.** Determine the activities and concurrent demand that
space must support, including shared and support functions. A building type is a
hypothesis for questions, not a prescribed plan, unit mix or dimension. Different
uses in a mixed program can have different users, operating periods and access
boundaries. An early program does not establish compliance or feasibility.

**Inputs.** Capture purpose, site and context, users, activities, operating hours,
capacity, desired qualities, budget, schedule, phasing and future change where
known. For each input identify its source, date and status: confirmed project
requirement, preference, assumption or open question. Describe equipment,
storage, servicing, privacy, environmental needs and access. Use persons,
activities, seats or other explicit capacity units; distinguish a population over
a period from the number present simultaneously.

1. For each user group, trace the activities and their duration, frequency and
   time of day or season. Include arrival, waiting, support and departure.
2. Identify normal and credible peak scenarios. State whether groups overlap,
   whether seats or rooms are shared and any turnover or changeover time. An
   unknown overlap remains unknown; do not sum nonconcurrent populations as a
   peak or assume sharing without operational evidence.
3. Translate each scenario into quantities of spaces and capacity per space.
   Identify functions that can share a room only when their uses, access,
   equipment, setup and timing are compatible. Keep the capacity basis separate
   from statutory occupancy, which requires its applicable source.
4. Test support demand and competing needs with the people who operate and use
   the building. Record unresolved conflicts and the decision maker. Quiet study
   and a busy public route through the same room, for example, may require a
   different route, buffer or explicit compromise.

**Small check.** Two groups of 20 people use a room at different times; a third
of 10 overlaps each group. The observed peak is 30, not 50, if those time and
sharing assumptions are confirmed. If the first two groups might overlap, test
that scenario separately rather than selecting a convenient peak.

## How can areas be counted once and compared?

**Decision and boundary.** Establish a consistent area basis and expose missing
allowances before using a total for a fit or option comparison. Define the
measurement boundary, units and inclusions. GFA, GIA and NIA depend on their
measurement conventions and are not interchangeable local legal categories.
When a formal metric is needed, identify the governing published method and
version. Do not silently convert one into a statutory area.

1. Give each physical space a stable ID, activity, quantity, capacity, unit area
   and total, source or assumption, confidence, and special relationships. For
   known values, total = quantity × unit area, with area in the same units.
   An unknown area stays blank or is an explicitly labeled working allowance
   agreed for the study; it is not a standard.
2. Assign each physical area once to a defined category. Identify whether room
   circulation is already included in each unit area; distinguish it from shared
   corridors, lobbies, stairs, lifts, plant, shafts and structural elements.
   A shared room appears once as a physical area even when several user groups
   use it. Note its allocations separately without summing them again.
3. Add only mutually exclusive components inside the chosen boundary. If a
   combined core allowance already includes a lobby or shaft, do not add that
   component again. If using a factor instead of explicit components, state its
   denominator and precisely what it covers; do not add covered items separately.
4. Show a subtotal of known areas and list missing components. A range is useful
   only if its assumptions and endpoints are clear. A ratio can check a defined
   relationship, but cannot turn an incomplete schedule into a measured total.
5. For floor fit, compare the assigned area plus uncovered circulation and core
   components with available area on the *same* boundary. A positive arithmetic
   remainder does not prove the shape, access or structure works; test those
   spatially. See [calculators](calculators.md) for calculation scope.

**Small check.** Two rooms at 20 m² each plus one shared room at 30 m² total
70 m² of listed rooms. If a 10 m² lobby is included in the core allowance, adding
it again as circulation overstates the total by 10 m². If the core area is
unknown, 70 m² is a partial subtotal, not the building total.

## Which relationships must the plan satisfy?

**Decision and boundary.** Translate operational relationships into constraints
that a spatial option can actually satisfy. Start at department or zone scale;
resolve internal room relationships separately where useful. A proximity label
alone does not describe the required connection.

1. List the relevant spaces or zones on both axes of an adjacency table, or use
   equivalent pairwise notes. For each significant pair record the reason,
   source and status: essential, desirable, neutral, undesirable or prohibited.
   A prohibition or other hard requirement cannot be traded away by a score.
2. Specify the relationship: direct door or shared boundary; short route; same
   level; visual supervision; acoustic or other separation; controlled access;
   shared service interface; or vertical connection. A visual link can coexist
   with acoustic separation; a controlled connection is not open public access.
   Define any quantitative distance only from project evidence or an applicable
   source, with its route and units. Mark unknowns.
3. Examine each row for missing support links and each pair for conflicts. If A
   needs both B and C close but B and C must be separated, test a buffer, separate
   approaches, a vertical connection or a duplicated service. Compare operational
   cost, staff, distance and access implications; do not silently drop a hard
   separation to obtain a neat cluster.
4. Translate relationships into zones and test a bubble or 2D block arrangement.
   Trace each essential connection and separation, including those across floors.
   The diagram communicates intent; it is not a dimensional or compliance check.
   Validate priorities and compromises with the relevant users and decision maker.

**Small check.** A reception must see both a public waiting area and a restricted
work area, while visitors must not enter the latter. A glazed, controlled boundary
can satisfy visibility and separation; a shared open doorway cannot satisfy both.

## How do people and goods move through access zones?

**Decision and boundary.** Choose route and access concepts that support actual
users and operations. Consider public, staff, residents, visitors, goods, waste,
maintenance and emergency movement separately where relevant; a route concept
is not a capacity calculation or an egress approval.

1. For each movement, follow origin → entrance → control point → destination →
   exit, noting hours, frequency, peak concurrence, load or equipment and ability
   to use changes in level. Include delivery, waste removal and after-hours use.
   Identify which steps or flows are unknown.
2. Assign public, shared, controlled and private zones with transitions appropriate
   to the use. Check who can cross each boundary, how access changes after hours,
   and whether independent access is needed. A publicly accessible meeting room
   need not open the entire staff zone when the rest of the building is closed.
3. Overlay flows and inspect crossings and queues: public arrival against goods,
   clean against waste, and quiet destinations against noisy or busy routes.
   Separate them in space or time where necessary; if they share a route, state
   the operating condition and test the peak, supervision and consequence of
   disruption. Do not assume an operational separation will always be maintained.
4. Walk each route in the proposal, including a step-free route concept, arrival,
   waiting, decision points, vertical connections and destination. Use sightlines,
   thresholds and recognizable destinations to aid orientation; test sightlines
   at route junctions and views from the vertical circulation landing. Signs alone
   do not repair an ambiguous layout. Identify exits and accessibility/fire questions
   for verification under [requirements](requirements.md).
5. Compare route patterns against the brief: a single-loaded route can offer
   external exposure but needs a longer edge; double-loaded can consolidate
   movement but may give the route less daylight and limit cross-aspect layouts.
   A courtyard or loop may aid orientation and outdoor connection while adding
   route length. Local climate, privacy, site and operation determine the trade.

## How should the program occupy floors and adapt over time?

**Decision and boundary.** Test a provisional arrangement in section and plan,
not a default floor assignment. Use the site, access points, floor boundaries,
area schedule, relationship priorities, outdoor exposure, access needs, service
interfaces and phase assumptions. Keep unknown geometry and engineering needs
visible; coordinating an interface does not size an engineering system.

1. Place functions with confirmed external access or operational dependencies
   first in each option. Test alternative floor assignments for the rest; avoid
   assuming that a particular use must always occupy a particular floor. Compare
   public access, deliveries, privacy, daylight, noise and routes between floors.
2. Reserve space for vertical circulation and service interfaces through the
   relevant floors. A central core may shorten some routes and leave perimeter
   space but constrain subdivision; a side core may free a continuous floor area
   while increasing travel or exposing an edge. Test the specific plate, not a
   generic efficiency ranking. Align related wet or service zones where useful,
   while checking maintenance access and conflicts with occupied spaces.
3. Compare each floor's assigned spaces and uncovered allowances to its available
   area under the same definition. Then test shape, entrances, route continuity,
   daylight exposure, quiet/noisy interfaces and connections to outdoors. Flag
   changes of structural layout, floor heights or major services for coordination.
4. For housing, compare unit mix, private/shared thresholds, single or multiple
   aspects and shared access against the actual occupants' needs. A gallery can
   support through-aspect units yet affect privacy at the access edge; a compact
   corridor can increase shared access efficiency while limiting some aspects.
   Test how changing the mix affects doors, shared access, acoustic boundaries
   and service demand. Do not infer local dwelling classifications or minimum
   sizes from typology.
5. Test a named future scenario: for example, a room subdivides, two functions
   combine, or a public room operates independently after hours. Identify which
   partitions, access boundaries, daylight, shared services and support spaces
   would need to change, and which fixed constraints would prevent conversion.
   Flexibility has costs and is not established by an open plan alone. For phased
   work, test each phase's access, services and functioning independently.

## How should a bounded revision or comparison be judged?

Identify the accepted baseline, requested change, affected rooms, relationships,
flows, totals and interfaces. Preserve accepted facts and decisions outside that
boundary; do not reset the brief. Trace consequences through the schedule and
routes, and identify any new conflict or decision required. Recalculate affected
subtotals on the original area basis and mark uncertain consequences as such.

For a comparison, apply the same scenario, units, boundaries and criteria to
all options. First identify failed or unverified hard constraints, then compare
preferences such as route quality, privacy, daylight opportunity, adaptability,
operational burden and area. Explain qualitative judgments and any weights rather
than hiding a hard failure in a weighted score. Record assumptions, changes,
trade-offs, the preferred option and who must confirm it. State what evidence
would change the judgment and what remains unresolved; area or adjacency studies
alone do not establish code compliance or structural feasibility.
