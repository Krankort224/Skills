# Program and space

Use this reference to turn a brief into a traceable spatial proposal. Pair it with
[requirements](requirements.md), [composition and style](composition-and-style.md),
and [performance and coordination](performance-and-coordination.md).

## Capture the brief

- Record purpose, site and context, users, activities, capacity, operating hours,
  desired qualities, budget, schedule, phasing, and future change where known.
- Attribute facts to the client, survey, or other source and date; distinguish
  confirmed requirements from preferences, assumptions, and open questions.
- Identify primary and support functions, equipment, storage, servicing, privacy,
  environmental needs, access, and how use changes over a day or season.
- A building type is a starting hypothesis, not a dimensional prescription.
  Mixed uses may have different users, entrances, hours, and access boundaries.
- Record conflicts and the decision maker: for example, a request for both quiet
  study and a lively public route through the same room.

## Space schedule

Give each space an ID, activity, user or capacity, quantity, unit area and total,
source or assumption, and confidence. Add key relationships and special needs.
For a first pass, an unknown area may remain blank or be an explicitly labeled
working allowance agreed for the option study; never present it as a standard.

Sum like-defined areas and keep the arithmetic visible. Account separately for
shared circulation, stairs, lifts, plant, shafts, structural elements, and other
allowances; avoid counting the same space twice. Where numbers are missing, show
an incomplete total or a range with its basis, not a spurious precise total.

GFA, GIA, and NIA are labels used under different measurement conventions, not
interchangeable local legal categories. Define the boundary and inclusions for
each area schedule and identify the governing published measurement method and
version when a formal metric is needed. Do not silently translate any of them
into a local planning or statutory area. See [calculators](calculators.md) for
calculation scope and limitations.

## Relationships and organization

- Make an adjacency table with spaces on both axes. Mark direct, desirable,
  avoid, or no particular relationship; annotate reasons and conflicting needs.
- Distinguish physical adjacency from visual connection, controlled access,
  acoustic separation, and a merely short route. Validate with users.
- Map public, staff, service, goods, and emergency movement separately when
  relevant. Note crossings, access control, arrival, waiting, and wayfinding.
- Group related spaces into zones, then test a bubble diagram or 2D block plan.
  The diagram describes intent, not verified construction dimensions.
- Compare single- and double-loaded circulation, central or side cores, and
  courtyard or clustered arrangements only where they fit the brief and site.
  Each trades route length, daylight exposure, privacy, adaptability and area.
- Show the entrance, accessible route concept, vertical connections, service
  access, exits to be verified, and relationships to outdoors in each option.
- Examine daily and peak flows, dead ends, delivery conflicts, quiet and noisy
  boundaries, and whether the sequence is legible without relying on signs.

## Record the judgment

For each alternative list inputs, assumptions, derived areas, unmet or uncertain
requirements, qualitative experience and major trade-offs. State why one option
is preferred, who must confirm it, and what evidence would change that choice.
An early area or adjacency study does not establish code compliance or structural
feasibility. Leave a clear issue list for the next design stage.
