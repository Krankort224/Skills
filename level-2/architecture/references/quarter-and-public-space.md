# Quarter and public space

Use this reference for architectural relationships across several buildings or
plots: the open spaces that organize them, their addresses, ground-use balance,
shared journeys and occupation in stages. It supports bounded studies and
comparisons, not a complete quarter design. Keep surveyed conditions, ownership,
project decisions, assumptions and applicable restrictions distinct. Missing
parcel or operating evidence permits conditional alternatives, not invented facts.

Start with the question being decided. The [activity program](program/demand.md#what-activities-and-capacity-must-the-program-accommodate)
owns demand and sharing; the [area ledger](program/area-ledger.md#how-can-areas-be-counted-once-and-compared)
owns metric definitions and single counting; [access zones](space/circulation/access-and-closure.md#how-do-people-and-goods-move-through-access-zones)
own journeys and [closure boundaries](space/circulation/access-and-closure.md#make-a-closed-zone-a-complete-boundary).
Here, connect those decisions across plots and common ground. Use
[site and context](site-and-context.md) for an individual edge, threshold and
building/ground strategy, and [housing and court](housing-and-court.md) for the
home-to-court relationship. Select jurisdiction and source applicability through
[requirements](requirements.md); none of the examples below supplies a statutory
ratio, density, catchment, route dimension or technical approval.

## Organize several plots around an open-space hierarchy

**Decision and inputs.** Decide which common spaces organize the whole and which
serve a local group. Establish the study boundary, actual surrounding destinations
and approaches, plot/control boundaries, retained features, ground levels,
program groups, intended public access and any accepted connections. Separate a
legal parcel from a proposed operating area: a desired crossing of another plot
is an unresolved dependency until its use and control are established.

1. Place the destinations and necessary external connections before dividing the
   remainder into building plots. Describe a journey from the surrounding public
   network into the proposal, across it if required, and back out. A line aimed
   at a neighboring gate proves little if that gate closes or leads nowhere.
2. Give each significant open space a job, users, operating period, controlling
   party and relationship to an occupied edge. Distinguish a public connector,
   quarter gathering place, local shared court, quiet garden, private outdoor
   extension and service/maintenance ground. Public ownership, public access,
   visual openness and a public activity are different attributes.
3. Choose a primary spatial relationship, then test a materially different one
   at the same demand and boundary. A hierarchy can use position, recognizable
   arrival, enclosure, activity and sequence; size alone does not establish it.
4. Assign each building a principal address and show which side serves its local
   court or private uses. Develop the intervening spaces as connected rooms and
   routes, including where a public journey stops. An empty interval is not
   automatically a park, a court or a usable connection.
5. Check a whole-site journey and a local journey in plan and section. Identify
   every boundary, level change, crossing and decision point. Check that a local
   quiet space does not become the unavoidable shortcut to the principal public
   destination. Revise plot division, entries or the organizing space if it does.

| Organizing alternative | Opportunity | Coupled cost and repair test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Common center with several addressed edges | A shared destination can unite different buildings and orient arrival. | Several paths, queues and events can overload the center. Move through movement to an edge or provide a bypass; do not simply enlarge the central label. Test how the far buildings work when the center is closed. |
| Public spine with local courts branching from it | A recognizable route can connect external destinations while each group has a quieter interior. | The ends may lack a destination; a long frontage may alternate activity with service backs. Test both ends, each crossing and branch threshold. Shorten, bend or give the route a genuine terminal activity when justified. |
| Linked clusters with several small centers | Different groups or level bands can have their own character and occupation schedule. | Equally emphasized places can be hard to distinguish; links may become exposed residual strips. Give clusters recognizable entries and a common ordering cue; compare additional route and maintenance burden. |
| Repeated plots on a common grid or street framework | Clear repeatable addresses and crossings can permit local variation and later change. | Repetition can ignore topography, a retained feature or a major public destination. Break or adapt the framework where that relationship matters; record the resulting nonstandard plot rather than forcing it back into the module. |

**Bounded comparison.** Assume three separately operated buildings must reach an
existing north entrance and a proposed east public destination. Option A brings
all three through a central event court. Option B keeps a public north-to-east
route along the court edge, with three local branches. A may give a stronger
shared arrival; B can keep everyday passage available during an event. If the
only door of building C still opens inside the event enclosure, B has not solved
independent access. Move that door to a connected edge or retain the access
conflict explicitly. These are relationship alternatives, without assumed site
geometry, crowd capacity or a claim that either layout fits.

A symmetrical composition can hide unequal access or poorly addressed backs.
Conversely, several different building forms can form a coherent place through
aligned entrances, a continuous public edge and a clear common space. Check the
actual relationship rather than requiring visual uniformity. The selected
hierarchy remains conditional on privacy, exposure, access rights and operation.

## Give mixed activities usable addresses

**Decision and inputs.** Place activities where their public presence, support,
privacy and operating periods can work together. Use the canonical activity
program to establish actual demand; a desired mixture is not evidence of market
demand, service capacity or a prescribed land-use percentage. Record each
operator's hours, arrival/queue, indoor/outdoor spill, storage, servicing, waste,
noise sensitivity and control boundary. Unknown tenancy or hours requires stated
scenarios rather than a promise of a continuously active frontage.

1. Relate each activity to its appropriate audience and route: passing public,
   booked visitor, local resident, supervised group or service worker. Give it
   an identifiable entrance from a route available when it operates. A tenancy
   name on an elevation does not establish a usable ground-floor plan.
2. Test at least the consequential daytime and evening/closed conditions.
   Locate arrivals and waiting, doors and outgoing users, deliveries and waste.
   Check what an observer actually encounters on each side of the open space.
3. Place compatible shared resources only where their access, timing and
   management can be confirmed. Preserve support for the use that remains open
   when its neighbor closes. Carry any new waiting, storage or lobby reservation
   into the program and area ledger.
4. Shape occupied edges with doors, inhabited windows, thresholds and deliberate
   outdoor extensions. Give sensitive rooms a suitable buffer, change of level,
   orientation or more private edge where needed. A buffer must not sever the
   only public journey or consume another operator's support space.
5. Walk the addressed edge from both real approaches during each scenario. Check
   whether people can identify their destination without entering the wrong
   court, and whether doors/queues/service activity displace the intended public
   use. Reposition the use, entrance or service interface before treating facade
   transparency as the correction.

Concentrating public uses at one node can support a recognizable destination and
shared arrival, but also concentrates closing hours, noise and deliveries.
Distributing small uses along a route can give several addresses, but requires
more separate support, longer servicing and credible activity between them.
Putting quieter uses behind a public band can protect them, but cannot leave
them dependent on a locked shop. Compare these arrangements with the same
activity/support demand; do not gain apparent efficiency by omitting back-of-house
space from the dispersed option.

**Counterexample and repair.** Assume a workshop and an evening meeting room
share a daytime reception building; the meeting room is reached only through the
workshop's controlled yard. Opening the meeting room's door onto that yard does
not give it an independent public address. Test an entrance from the common
route with its own controllable arrival/support, or an explicitly agreed shared
access zone whose boundary protects the workshop when closed. The latter may
need new partitions or a changed yard boundary, not merely a booking rule. Check
noise and departure against nearby homes separately. Neither option proves fire,
accessibility or acoustic compliance.

## Reconcile ground use without double counting

**Decision and inputs.** Establish whether the same piece of ground has been
promised to incompatible uses or counted twice across plots. Retain the canonical
area definitions. State the exact horizontal study boundary and inclusions,
units, measurement date, source geometry and any excluded land. Keep building
footprints, floor areas, legal plot totals, accessible landscape and operational
activity areas in separate ledgers; equal units do not make them interchangeable.

1. Partition the study ground into nonoverlapping, identified base pieces. Use
   whichever base classes answer the question: building footprint, service,
   movement, public forecourt, shared court, planted ground, retained/unavailable
   or future-phase reservation. Draw or describe shared boundaries consistently.
   Include residuals and unknown pieces instead of silently making them useful.
2. Cross-tabulate those same pieces by plot or controlling party if required.
   Common ground has one owner in the balance, even when several buildings use
   it. Reconcile both the class totals and plot/common totals to the same whole;
   adding the two views together would count the site twice.
3. Add activity, access, surface, canopy, level and time as attributes or overlays.
   A paved forecourt used for a market is still one base piece; a tree canopy
   over a path does not create a second piece of ground. A landscaped roof,
   basement below and footprint above require separate level/basis accounts.
4. Test whether each claimed activity fits its contiguous available shape after
   actual movement, access, supports and control reservations. Treat a sliver,
   fenced strip or steep bank as such; its area alone does not supply an outdoor
   activity. Identify any simultaneous overlap and the operating decision needed
   to share it at different times.
5. After a revision, transfer area between the responsible pieces and reconcile
   again. Keep the footprint/floor/land definitions fixed across alternatives.
   Do not count a shared court once in every plot or subtract a reserved facility
   plot and then subtract the same facility footprint from the remainder.

**Original illustrative balance.** Assume a 10,000 m² horizontal study boundary;
all figures are trial reservations, with no prescribed ratios or formal land
measurement claim. Its nonoverlapping base pieces are:

| Base ground class | Trial area, m² |
| --- | ---: |
| Building footprints | 2,400 |
| Service ground | 900 |
| Through routes | 1,400 |
| Public forecourt | 900 |
| Local shared court | 1,400 |
| Planted ground | 2,200 |
| Unavailable future-phase reservation | 800 |
| **Same study boundary** | **10,000** |

Suppose the forecourt's simultaneous event arrangement reserves 500 m² for the
event, 200 m² for waiting and 200 m² for its internal passage: 900 m², all inside
that forecourt rather than added to the site total. A newly required 200 m²
loading reservation at the same time cannot be credited as free sharing. If it
is permanently reassigned from the forecourt, service becomes 1,100 m² and the
forecourt 700 m²; the ground total remains 10,000 m², but the 900 m² arrangement
fails by 200 m². One conditional revision reduces event space to 300 m² while
retaining the two 200 m² reservations. Another changes servicing time only if
operation and its spatial holding needs can support that choice. Neither equal
arithmetic nor an area reduction proves usable shape, crowd capacity or a viable
event. Test those separately and report the reduced activity consequence.

Do not derive floor area by dividing this ground remainder by an unexplained
factor. Mixed heights require actual per-building floor assumptions and a
separate floor ledger. Territorial percentages, service provision and density
require their own project/jurisdiction basis, not imported coursework indicators.

## Keep public, private and service journeys continuous

**Decision and inputs.** Resolve connections across buildings, plots, common
ground and external approaches. Extend the canonical access/closure method;
do not create a second access classification. For each journey retain the user
or load, origin/destination, hours, control points, level changes, operator and
relevant verified geometry. Include residents/visitors, through public users,
booked groups, goods, waste, maintenance and emergency spatial interfaces where
relevant. A colored line is route intent, not a transport or egress calculation.

1. Trace a complete chain through every affected plot and public/common edge.
   Record the source of any right to cross another party's land. A school,
   nursery or private court cannot act as a public link just because its gate is
   drawn open in one plan.
2. Locate encounters between journeys: a delivery crossing a public spine,
   event queue at a dwelling entrance, waste beside seating, or maintenance
   through a controlled garden. Test actual simultaneous operation and where
   people or loads wait; line intersections do not reveal the whole conflict.
3. Compare separation by location, distinct levels, a protected/common interface,
   or confirmed time management. Different levels add vertical journeys and
   maintenance burdens. Time management needs an operator, enforceable period
   and a response to delayed arrival; it does not remove physical holding needs.
4. Close each controlled operator and event enclosure in turn, following the
   canonical closure method. Also remove a significant shared link for planned
   maintenance where credible. Trace the remaining open destinations and service
   obligations. Mark a lost journey as a failure instead of drawing a route
   through the closed zone or inventing a right across a neighbor.
5. Reconcile corrections with open-space hierarchy and ground use. A bypass can
   consume a quiet court or split a gathering place; a relocated service entrance
   can produce a blank public edge. Retest those consequences and the continuous
   inclusive journey using [spatial coordination](space/circulation/inclusive-and-emergency-interfaces.md#inclusive-use-and-fire-spatial-coordination).

**Bounded failure.** Assume buildings A and B must remain available while an
intermediate managed garden is locked after 20:00. A direct path across it is
shorter, but B has no other connection to the public approach. Option 1 keeps a
public edge strip outside the garden closure, relocating the fence and B's
threshold; its width, levels, security and ownership still need evidence.
Option 2 provides a different public approach to B, if that connection exists
and can be controlled appropriately. Merely leaving B's door unlocked does not
repair the missing path. Compare actual detour, exposure, maintenance and public
legibility, without assigning an unsupported acceptable walking distance.

A circle drawn around a facility indicates geometric proximity only. Trace the
real route to the actual entrance through crossings, boundaries and levels before
claiming connectivity; availability and suitability of the facility are further
questions. This method neither establishes service catchment compliance nor
sizes roads, crossings, turning areas, parking, loading or emergency access.
Reserve unresolved interfaces for their applicable evidence and specialists.

## Make each phase an operable place

**Decision and inputs.** Decide whether an occupied stage can function while the
remainder is unavailable. Establish the intended occupation sequence, plot/control
boundaries, retained uses, essential program/support, public connections and any
confirmed shared infrastructure dependencies. A construction budget or a colored
phase outline does not establish physical independence.

1. For every stage list occupied buildings, usable common ground, unavailable
   future plots and construction/working territory. Treat unbuilt destinations,
   paths, support rooms and landscape as absent, not as completed promises.
2. Assign each stage a complete architectural chain: external approach, address,
   usable threshold, required inclusive journey, intended outdoor activity,
   service/waste/maintenance access, access control and spatial emergency
   interfaces. Identify which shared support must already exist. Do not declare
   independence while relying on a future core, unfinished link or inaccessible
   service room.
3. Map dependencies as prerequisite → occupied use → consequence if delayed.
   A shared energy, water or other utility connection is a specialist dependency
   to confirm, not permission to design or size it here. Distinguish spatially
   independent operation from unverified structural, utility, legal and technical
   commissioning independence.
4. Compare completing the shared public route/support first, completing one
   self-contained building and local space first, or providing an explicitly
   temporary connection/support. The first commits early common work; the second
   may duplicate facilities or weaken the eventual center; temporary work needs
   a usable footprint, maintenance, removal and replacement sequence.
5. Test the boundary while the next stage is being built: separate occupied
   journeys from work territory, show temporary secure edges and reserve any
   affected access without assuming a technical construction solution. Check
   whether the next stage can be approached without crossing the occupied court.
   Flag unresolved construction logistics for specialist review.
6. Test conversion to the next stage as well as its final state. Identify any
   closure or relocation, where temporary elements go, and which occupied use
   loses access during the transition. Reconcile each stage's ground ledger;
   an unavailable future plot cannot also supply its occupied neighbor's garden.

**Before/after dependency test.** Assume stage 1 opens building A while B and the
central square remain unavailable. The initial proposal gives A a temporary door
but its only approach and waste collection route cross the future square's work
territory. A is not spatially independent. One revision completes a permanent
edge approach and service interface before A opens, and gives A a local outdoor
space outside later work. Another delays occupation until the square and its
required connections are complete. Compare early work, temporary cost, loss of
usable ground and future disruption. Do not claim the revision is constructible
or commissioned until its technical dependencies are confirmed.

Phases can have different temporary edges while retaining a coherent eventual
hierarchy. Give each occupied stage a recognizable front and a cared-for boundary;
calling unfinished land “future park” cannot supply present amenity. Record the
condition that would prevent occupation and the person responsible for resolving
it. Architectural completeness is necessary evidence, not permission to occupy.

## Test the whole site in season and section

**Decision and inputs.** Decide which spaces and journeys remain useful in the
actual operating conditions, and how several plots meet one ground system. Use
verified levels, exposure, retained landscape, water-path evidence and operating
plans when supplied. Otherwise label test conditions explicitly. Do not infer
slope, flood behavior, soil capability, species suitability or clearing practice
from an attractive site image or another project's climate.

1. Select consequential scenarios from intended use: an ordinary open day, a
   dark/closed period, a locally relevant cold/wet/hot season, landscape
   establishment, and maintenance or partial closure where relevant. Define which
   spaces are expected to work in each; not every lawn or terrace needs the same
   operating promise.
2. Trace the network of usable destinations in each scenario. Test access to
   entrances, waiting, seating choice, exposed crossings and working/maintenance
   ground. A summer connection through a water feature or seasonal garden cannot
   silently remain the only all-season approach. Locate where displaced furniture
   or cleared material is held if the operating brief includes that work.
3. Read successive ground sections across plot boundaries: external approach →
   common route → threshold → occupied floor → local outdoor space and neighboring
   ground. Record actual or assumed levels at the interfaces. Compare following
   level bands, stepped groups or a common platform with the individual building
   methods in [site and context](site-and-context.md#let-topography-and-servicing-shape-the-section).
4. Check cumulative consequences across the whole: repeated level changes,
   long detours, exposed intervals, inaccessible maintenance pockets, abrupt
   neighboring ground and conflicts with observed/proposed water routes. Record
   retaining, below-ground, support and drainage dependencies without sizing
   them. A level threshold within each plot does not prove a continuous journey
   between plots.
5. Treat landscape as changing occupied space. Test initial planting and a stated
   later condition, seasonal leaf loss, views, boundary legibility, cleaning and
   replacement access. Temporary screening may be needed if privacy depends on
   growth; required access/security boundaries cannot depend on assumed foliage.
   A planted bank or pond has its own access and maintenance interface, not just
   a decorative color in the ground balance.
6. Change geometry or operation where a scenario fails: relocate a pause off an
   exposed path, move a seasonal attraction beside an enduring route, alter
   entrance levels, keep a maintenance strip, split a platform or revise the
   intended season. Recount reservations and retest the affected addresses,
   closures and stages. Exposure diagrams alone do not demonstrate comfort.

**Original section test.** Assume a common route reaches plot A at level +100.00 m
and plot B at +101.20 m, with an assumed 30.00 m horizontal separation. The level
difference is 1.20 m; a single straight connection over that horizontal distance
would have an average rise/run of 1.20/30.00 = 0.04, or 4%. This is a diagnostic
of assumed geometry, not an acceptable route gradient or a designed ramp.
Local sections, landings, crossfall, thresholds and the actual journey remain
unknown. Compare an approach along a verified level band, adjustment of the entry
levels where possible, or a separately resolved level-changing journey. Do not
replace that work with two locally flat entrance details. If a proposed winter
closure removes the level-band route, repeat the network test for what remains.

At a seasonal pond beside that connection, a frozen or drained condition changes
its usable edge and maintenance needs; it does not convert the water footprint
into guaranteed path or event area. At a new planted screen, the first occupied
season may have different privacy and enclosure from the intended mature state.
Record the stage/season each claim describes and the operating input that could
reverse the preferred arrangement. No hydrology, earthwork quantity, retaining
calculation, landscape specification or prediction of microclimate is supplied.
